# Research: classic RPG Maker psychological horror

Raw findings from 9 research passes (one per game or craft topic). Sources are listed per section. Note: several researchers could only reach some sites; Ao Oni findings come partly from decoding the game data in a public GitHub repo.


## Ao Oni (noprops, RPG Maker XP; first version 2004, public versions from 2008, final official v6.23 in 2012): mansion, chase system, puzzles, versions, scares, saving, endings

Network note: WebFetch could not resolve any host except github.com (DNS lookup failed for wikipedia, fandom, tvtropes, steam, speedrun.com, medium and others). This looks like the environment's network policy. To get primary data anyway, I decoded the actual event data of Ao Oni v5.2 (the English build translated by Sephy, as hosted in the public GitHub repo nolife99/ao-oni). I used a safe pure-Python Ruby-Marshal reader covering all 73 maps, 51 common events, 39 items, 176 switch names and the embedded Ruby scripts. So every mechanic below marked as v5.2 data is the real logic, not fan guesswork. Web context (versions, reception, essays, 6.23 puzzles, adaptations) comes only from search-engine extracts of the listed pages. The core finding is that Ao Oni's fear is cheap systems, carefully tuned. There is one same-speed, greedy-pathing monster that follows you through the exact door you used. Chases run on a countdown the player never sees. Random ambush rolls are rationed by a 5-minute cooldown. Closet hiding is resolved entirely by sound, with a small random chance of death. Scripted chases are attached to the moment you grab a reward. The puzzles are long multi-floor item chains, many with codes randomized per playthrough, and they drag you across the house through the monster's rooms. The game is silent except for the chase music.


### Mechanics

- **Global chase state with an invisible countdown (v5.2 data)**: One switch ('Ao Oni appears') activates a dormant oni event that exists in every room. Starting a chase does five things: it records which room the oni is in, disables the menu (so no saving or items), starts the chase BGM 'horor-b' and starts a countdown. There are 34 timed chases in v5.2, from 7 s to 25 s: mostly 9, 10 or 15 s, with 19-20 s for the first library chase and 25 s for the lattice chase. The timer sprite is still in the scripts, but its draw call was removed, so the player never sees a number. A parallel common event ends the chase only when two things are true: the timer is at 1 s or less, AND the player is on a different map than the oni. It then stores the current play time as 'last chase', fades the music and re-enables the menu. *Why it works:* The player cannot count down. The only readable rule is 'keep moving until the music fades'. Because ending a chase requires changing rooms, every chase becomes a dash through the house rather than a stand-off. With no visible meter, the threat feels organic rather than gamey.
- **Door-follow transitions: it comes in through the door you used (v5.2 data)**: Every room has an 'oni handler' parallel event. When you enter during a chase, it reads which room the oni was in. It waits a delay tuned per door (7-35 frames, roughly 0.2-0.9 s). Some rooms add a 50% chance of a further 50 frames, so the follow time is unpredictable. It then teleports the oni onto the doorway tile you came through, plays a door-open sound and updates the oni's room ID. If you are still standing on that tile, it is instant game over. Staircases have a kill rectangle: if the oni is anywhere inside the stairwell area when you step on the stairs, you die. Special room codes cover other entrances: '100' means the oni is already standing inside the room at a scripted spot (used for item-triggered chases), and '200' means a crash entrance with a cannon/impact sound. *Why it works:* Doors become timing tests and reliable information: the door sound tells you it is coming and from where. The short delay gives a half-second breather after each door, then panic. Players learn never to linger just inside a doorway. Reviewers' complaints about being caught on stairs while 'barely touching' come from the stairwell rectangle.
- **Same-speed greedy pursuit plus hand-placed 'unstick' corrections (v5.2 data)**: The oni's route is a repeating 'attack_on' script plus RPG Maker's built-in 'move toward player'. That logic steps along the axis with the larger gap and tries the other axis if blocked. It moves at speed 4 with frequency 6, the same speed as the player. There is no pathfinding, so furniture traps it; this is the source of the fan advice to circle a table or couch until the chase expires. Rooms with problem geometry (library bookshelves, tatami room, nursery, Takeshi's room, the cell) have 'pos correction' parallel events. These compare the oni's and the player's tiles and switch the oni to a page that forces a fixed direction (up, right, down or left) so it does not get stuck in silly spots. Scripted, unescapable kills set the oni's speed to 8, faster than the player. Contact is resolved by a small action-battle add-on (XRXS/XAS 'battler attachment'): a touch sets the game-over switch. *Why it works:* At equal speed, survival comes from routing and room knowledge, not reflexes. Greedy AI is learnable and exploitable, which empowers players, while the hand-placed nudges stop the monster from looking dumb where it matters. Faster scripted versions signal 'this one is a cutscene death'.
- **Rationed random ambushes: per-room rolls behind a 5-minute cooldown (v5.2 data)**: 15 rooms contain a 'rand oni' parallel event. It does nothing while a chase is active or until 300 s of play time have passed since the last chase ended. Once eligible, it waits until the player steps into a trigger zone (for example a specific row near a closet), then rolls 1-10. The spawn chance is 70% in most rooms, 80% in the 2F/3F halls and 90% in the library, tatami room and basement archive. On a failed roll the event erases itself until the room is reloaded. On success, a closet or fusuma visibly slides open (or a door sound plays) and a chase starts with a 10-19 s timer. 'Last chase' is initialised to 3600 at the start, so random spawns effectively begin only after the first scripted chase. Even the scripted piano chase checks the cooldown, so chases cannot come back to back. *Why it works:* In the essay's words, 'every screen we walk through triggers another fresh roll of the die'. The cooldown also guarantees a quiet window to think and solve puzzles. The result is constant dread with controlled interruption, and the player cannot tell the two apart.
- **Closet hiding resolved purely by sound (v5.2 data)**: Examining a wardrobe during a chase plays the doors-opening animation, makes the player invisible and calls a 'Closet hide' routine. First, the timer stops and the menu unlocks. If the oni is already in the same room, a full-screen picture of the closet doors sliding apart plays, then game over. Otherwise the screen goes black and the routine rolls 0-9. After 40 frames a door sound plays (the oni enters). A roll of 0 (10%; 30% in one late-game room) means it finds you and you die. Otherwise a creature-breathing loop plays for 80 frames (plus 40 more on odd rolls), then a second door sound (it leaves), and you press a key to come out. On exiting, rolls of 1-2 (a further 20%) restart a 10 s chase with the oni standing in the room; otherwise the chase ends. *Why it works:* Black screen, two door sounds and breathing let imagination do the work, and fans repeat the rule 'wait for the second door'. The randomized find chance and the 'it's still here' outcome stop hiding from being a free win. It also teaches the real skill: break line of sight and change rooms before you hide.
- **One-hit death; save anywhere except during chases (v5.2 data)**: The custom menu has only three entries: Items, Save and Quit. Saving is allowed anywhere, but the menu is disabled for every chase. A touch from the oni goes to a black picture and Game Over. There is no health, and the leftover RPG Maker HP/MP stats are hidden in later versions. *Why it works:* Stakes are total, but how much you lose depends on your own saving habits. Players develop a 'save after every step' ritual, which keeps them anxious. A reviewer of the sequel complained about exactly this ritual, so tune how often the monster appears accordingly.
- **Item use by map + exact tile + facing, chosen from the menu (v5.2 data)**: Using an item from the inventory calls a common event that checks the current map ID, the exact tile and the facing direction. Examples: the soaked handkerchief works only on the piano room tile (8,8) facing right; the + screwdriver tears the wallpaper only from tatami tile (9,13) facing up; the padlock fits the cell door only from (7,8) facing down or (7,10) facing up. Combining items works by using one item while holding the other: detergent + handkerchief gives a soaked handkerchief; oil into the lighter; lighter on the memo or the map to heat them; metal rod into a slotted screwdriver attachment; swapping screwdriver tips. Examining spots gives one-line hints ('There's a bulge on the ridge of the couch...', 'There's something wrong with the wallpaper...'). *Why it works:* It is cheap to author and makes every solution a deliberate trip: 'carry X to place Y'. That trip routes you through rooms that can roll an ambush. The one-line examine hints are what save it from pure pixel-hunting.
- **Full-screen close-up clue pictures that transform when you apply a tool (v5.2 data)**: Documents and key objects (memo, floor map, piano keyboard, red diary, torn page, abacus cipher, puppet, jewels) appear as full-screen pictures over a black backdrop until you press a key. Applying a tool swaps or animates the picture. Heating the map with the filled lighter reveals hidden lines. Heating the memo reveals hidden writing. Wiping the paint-caked piano with the soaked handkerchief reveals one of 5 marked keyboards. The torn page slides onto the diary page. The cipher slides into the map's frame. *Why it works:* Clues are readable despite tiny sprites, and the reveal animation is the puzzle's reward moment. It also gives the designer a second visual channel independent of the tile art.
- **Per-playthrough randomized codes (v5.2 data)**: In the opening cutscene the game rolls Piano Pattern (1-2), Keyboard (1-5), Cipher (1-5), Diary code (1-5) and Annex cipher pattern (1-5). Each lock accepts only the code for the rolled combination. The piano safe, for example, has 10 possible codes (9761, 6392, 1416, 6799, 5156 or 1971, 2632, 9146, 6679, 9516). The abacus cipher pictures are named with their answers (7351, 4265, 3976, 1284, 9164), and so are the annex cipher pictures (4775, 5625, 1435, 2685, 9315). *Why it works:* Players cannot paste a code from a walkthrough; they must read the clue system. Replays stay fresh. Walkthroughs and the repo README end up publishing method tables instead of answers.
- **Darkness, lighter radius and a power breaker (v5.2 data)**: Basement and annex rooms carry light variables (Basement light, Junk room light, Puzzle room light, light bulb, 2nd sinking light). While dark, examining things fails ('Too dark'). Using the lighter there shows a light-circle overlay picture and sets the room to lit. The circuit breaker restores basement power, which a safe needs, and touching it also starts a 10 s chase. A light bulb item fixes another room. *Why it works:* Darkness gates exploration behind a tool you earned upstairs and shrinks your view exactly where ambushes happen. It also escalates the mood from the dusty-but-lit main house into the dark lower levels.
- **Persistence punishment and foreshadowing fake-outs (v5.2 data)**: Foyer bathroom door: each try says 'The door won't open.' and increments a counter. On the 4th try before the library chase, the door opens by itself. Hiroshi walks in, the door shuts, a monster roar plays, the door sprite swaps to a blood-splattered version, and it is game over. Earlier, an animated shadow passes behind that same door's glass. In the library, the first visit plays a 'fake oni' that walks out from the shelves and vanishes with a sting; no chase follows. *Why it works:* These moments teach that the house is aware of you and that poking at things has consequences. The fake sighting builds anticipation before the first real chase. The bloody door stays as a landmark of the threat.
- **Companion gathering changes the escape (v5.2 data)**: Friends are found hiding: Takeshi in wardrobes on several floors (shivering, later joining), Mika in the nursery (refuses to follow until you have found an exit), Takuro in the library or archive. A party counter tracks who joined. At the rope-ladder exit, with fewer than 4 in the party the oni eats the ladder and you die. With all 4, the oni eats the ladder in a cutscene and you flee by another route. Another exit has the oni burst up through the shed floor, followed by a final chase with no timer. *Why it works:* It gives exploration an emotional goal beyond keys, makes the final escape feel earned, and turns 'rescue your friends' into a gating condition players discover through failure.

### Puzzle examples

- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Library key: a sparkle sits on top of a tall shelf in a small 3F room where Takeshi hides shivering in a wardrobe. Pushing the chair from the left or right side (two switch states) moves it under the sparkle; examining it from the chair gives the Library key. *Lesson:* Use a visual glint as the clue and the room's furniture as the tool, with no inventory needed. A scared NPC sitting in the same room sets the tone before the monster is ever seen.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Bedroom key in the library: the first visit plays a fake oni walk-by. Taking the book off a shelf reveals a glowing key behind it; picking it up turns the player around and starts the first scripted chase (20 s), with the oni already in the library. This also unlocks the bathroom, toilet and tatami doors and enables random spawns. *Lesson:* Attach the first real chase to the first satisfying reward, tease the monster beforehand, and use the chase as the hinge that opens the map.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) One-way drop into a locked room: in the 3F bedroom, taking the memo from the bed lets you push the bed aside, revealing a hole in the floor. Falling through lands you inside the locked 2F piano room, whose door then unlocks from the inside ('Door unlocked'), making it a permanent shortcut. *Lesson:* Enter a locked room from an unexpected direction, then convert the lock into a shortcut. Vertical connections make a small house feel layered.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Piano safe, the signature chain: the keys are 'caked with paint', and examining them the first time starts a 15 s chase. Detergent from the toilet's shelf plus Takeshi's handkerchief (left in his closet after the library chase) make a soaked handkerchief, used facing the piano to reveal one of 5 marked keyboards. Lighter (tatami closet) plus oil (2F side room) gives a working lighter, used to heat the memo and reveal one of 2 hidden instruction patterns. Pushing a bookshelf in the piano room reveals a wall safe. The keyboard pattern read through the memo gives the 4-digit code, and the safe yields the Nursery key. *Lesson:* One lock, five ingredients across four rooms and three floors, with the solution generated at runtime. The scare comes on the very first touch of the puzzle object, before the player even knows it is a puzzle.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Hidden basement door: the Map room yields a floor map of the 1st floor ('I wonder what these lines represent...'). Heating it with the lighter reveals marks pointing at the tatami room wall ('There's something wrong with the wallpaper...'). The + screwdriver, from a bulge in the nursery couch, tears off the wallpaper and exposes a door with no knob ('The doorknob is missing'). The doorknob comes from the attic room, which is unlocked by the attic key from draining the bathtub. Result: the basement. *Lesson:* A secret passage built from four items found on four different floors. Each step's examine text hints at the next verb, so the chain is long but always legible.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data + repo README)) Finger riddle, then abacus cipher, then code door: a painting in the 3F archive carries a rhyme about stretching things side by side and ordering them by length, small at the bottom and tall on top. It opens a room with five objects named thumb, index, middle, ring and pinky to arrange. Success gives the 'Cipher'. Using the cipher while standing at the frame where the map was found slides it over an abacus drawing; per the README, top dots touching the line add 5 and bottom dots add 1, giving a 4-digit number (1 of 5). That number opens a passcode door deep in the basement, and touching its panel the first time starts a chase coming up the stairs behind you. *Lesson:* Chain a riddle, a physical arrangement and a culture-specific decoding tool (the abacus). Put the final code entry in a corridor where the monster arrives, so applying the answer happens under pressure.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Diary overlay and powered safe: in the dark basement archive the chair must be shoved through several positions to reach a red diary on a high shelf. A torn page found later in the annex lines up with the diary (an overlay animation), giving one of 5 codes. The wall safe it opens is behind an iron plate screwed to the wall: you need a slotted screwdriver, made by turning a found metal rod into a '-' attachment, and the breaker must be on ('The power appears to be cut off...'). The reward is the Annex key. *Lesson:* Layer three independent requirements on one lock (code, tool, power) so the player keeps a mental checklist while navigating chase-prone rooms.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Reusable planks: the annex has holes in the floor and a limited number of planks (found in the bridge room, the hall and the dining room). You place a plank to cross and can 'Take the plank back?' to reuse it elsewhere. *Lesson:* A routing and resource puzzle. Deciding where to leave a plank is also deciding your escape lane in the next chase.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Puppet and jewels: a puppet with one red eye falls in a dark room. Red and blue jewels are found and swapped between a 'head' mechanism and the puppet's eyes. The assembled puppet's head is placed on a statue spot to receive the Third floor key. Elsewhere a puppet and a diary must be inserted into a furnace you light with the lighter. *Lesson:* Uncanny dolls make good puzzle props: the creepiness is the clue's packaging. Moving items between two receptacles teaches 'items can be taken back out'.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Colour field puzzle: 21 floor panels must be recoloured until 'None of the fields should have the same color as the ones next to it' (map colouring). Hiroshi says 'I know this sort of puzzle'. *Lesson:* A well-known logic puzzle drops in as a breather and needs no special knowledge, which suits a mid-game pacing dip.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Oikakekko ('tag') board: a turn-based pursuit puzzle on an 11-node graph. You move the blue piece one link per turn and the AI moves the yellow piece; you win by landing on it. After the AI's first move the game fires a one-time 'back attack': a full-screen close-up of the oni zooms at the camera, you are dropped back into the room and a 9 s chase begins. *Lesson:* An inversion (you are the chaser on the board) followed by an ambush in the middle of the puzzle. It is the clearest example of 'punish the moment the player is absorbed or relieved'.
- (Ao Oni v5.2 (event data)) Cell trap: a Star-piece glints inside a jail cell with a lattice door. A small key under the rug in the 'Astral' room opens a padlocked door and gives you the padlock. If you take the star without first padlocking the cell door, the oni bursts in at speed 8, flings the door open and kills you: a scripted, unavoidable death. With the padlock fitted, the same grab becomes a 7 s survivable chase. Star, Moon (from a puzzle room) and Sun (from a cave 4-digit lock) pieces then go into the Astral room. *Lesson:* An anticipation puzzle: the solution is to prepare before the obvious grab. It works once players have learned that pickups summon the monster. It is harsh but memorable, so put a save opportunity right before it.
- (Ao Oni v6.23 (Fandom walkthrough via search extract)) Annex basement: vinegar, received from Takuro hiding in a closet, reveals that the 'Red Key' is actually the 'Iron Gate Key'. Inside the jail are 3 paintings with buttons under them. A disc found under a rug after a Takeshi/Mika chase shows the colours to press, read clockwise. *Lesson:* Later versions replaced whole puzzle sets but kept the structure: an item transforms another item's identity, then a found diagram encodes an order. The friend-as-item-giver ties story beats to progress.
- (Ao Oni v6.23 (Fandom walkthrough via search extract)) Bedroom key moved from the library to a table in the right hallway (push away the green book); taking it still starts the first house-wide chase. The basement key sits on a high shelf reached by pushing a chair under a sparkle, and the jail cell is reached by using a lighter and a candle and then moving a bookshelf in the back room. *Lesson:* Even after a full rework the designer kept the same proven beats (reward triggers the first chase, sparkle plus chair, shelf hiding a door) and only rearranged locations. These are reusable verbs, not story-specific puzzles.

### Atmosphere and pacing

- Silence is the default: 71 of 73 v5.2 maps play no background music. The only real music is the chase loop 'horor-b' (started 37 times), so music means danger and its fade means safety. TV Tropes describes the chase track as a looping build-up of 'Psycho' strings, a drone and heartbeat.
- 48 of 73 maps are exactly 20x15 tiles, one 640x480 screen. You see the whole room and all its doors at once, which makes door sounds and entrances legible and lets you plan an escape loop around furniture.
- Core loop: explore, find an item, the item pickup or first touch triggers a scripted chase, then a guaranteed 5-minute quiet window (cooldown), then random ambush rolls on room entry. Tension never fully drops because you do not know the cooldown exists.
- Relief is punished. The design essay 'The Hunt for the Horror of Aooni' argues the puzzles pull you into scary spaces and anticipate when you will let your guard down: setup, reassurance, then the scare. The data matches: the bedroom key, the first piano touch, the star-piece grab and the middle of the oikakekko board puzzle all trigger the monster.
- Isolation is engineered in the first two minutes: four friends enter, one hears a noise, Hiroshi scoffs ('There can't be such things as ghosts, scientifically speaking') and goes alone. In the kitchen he finds a broken plate, and back in the foyer everyone has vanished ('Where did everyone go...?'). Exits are sealed.
- Fear is modelled by NPCs: Takeshi is repeatedly found shivering in wardrobes, which also teaches closets as hiding places before you need one.
- Tease before payoff: a non-chasing 'fake oni' walks across the library on the first visit and a shadow passes behind the bathroom door's glass before any chase exists.
- Escalation over the run: the main house is lit, then the basement is dark and needs the lighter and breaker, then the annex adds holes in the floor, dolls and crash entrances (impact sound effect). Late scripted kills move faster than the player, and the final chase has no timer at all.
- The menu lock during chases (no items, no saving) makes the chase feel like a different mode of play; reopening the menu is the 'all clear' signal.
- Hiding is pure audio drama: a black screen, a door opening, about 2-3 s of breathing, a door closing. Players invent the rule 'wait for two door sounds'.

### Story structure

- Premise (shared by all versions): Hiroshi and classmates Takuro, Takeshi and Mika visit an abandoned mansion on the outskirts of town rumoured to be haunted. v5.2 opens with the caption 'In the outskirts of the city, there's an uninhabited mansion. Rumor has it that the place is haunted...'. In 6.23 they are middle-school students instead of high-schoolers.
- Very little exposition. Plot advances through friend encounters gated by puzzles: Takeshi in closets on several floors, Takuro in the archive ('Let's split up... I'll look around on the first floor'), Mika in the nursery refusing to follow until an exit exists. Puzzle objects such as the red and black diaries and the torn page double as the only hints of the house's past.
- Friend deaths are staged as set pieces, not cutscenes you can avoid. v5.2 data has Takuro killed in the library by a fast oni, and the oni eating the rope ladder ('See eaten' switch) at an exit. 6.23 makes this the theme: per the Fandom wiki, the oni can turn people into its own kind, and the friends return as Takuro-, Takeshi- and Mika-Oni (Mika's death is discovered in a child's bedroom).
- v5.2 endings (event data): (1) Escape alone. Closing text says he never told anyone, never saw his companions again, and asks: 'What was the real identity of that monster? Is it still in there? Or is it maybe wandering about in the mountains?' (2) Escape via the shed, where the oni bursts up through the floor and chases you without a timer. This continues into a nightmare sequence (Takeshi trapped in a closet), a waking 'Real life' scene, a school scene where Takuro and Hiroshi wonder whether it was a bad dream, and an epilogue in which a new group enters the mansion ('Don't tell me you're afraid! Of ghosts?!'). It closes with a moral warning: never recklessly enter a mysterious mansion, especially if no one lives there anymore.
- Requirement-based branching: whether you gathered all four party members decides if the rope-ladder exit is survivable, so the 'good' escape is earned by exploration rather than dialogue choices (the only real choice in the data is Mika's 'Follow me / Wait here').
- Unanswered origin: the monster is never explained in v5.2. Ambiguity plus a cyclical epilogue (another group arrives) is the whole mythology. 6.23's ending reportedly shows an Ao Oni approaching and opening its mouth; fan wikis disagree on who it is.

### Art and audio

- Built in RPG Maker XP: 32x32 tiles, a 640x480 window, mostly stock RTP tilesets and sound effects (file names like 025-Door02, 017-Creature01, 052-Cannon01, 082-Monster04). The house reads as an ordinary Western/Japanese mix: wooden doors, bookshelves, a tatami room with sliding closets, bathrooms and a piano room.
- The monster is a single custom sprite ('ao'): a hulking, naked blue humanoid with an oversized, uncanny human face, animated with the standard 4-direction walk. Reviewers split between finding it terrifying and finding it 'more laughable than scary', but its blank stare is the series' icon.
- Custom sprite variants carry the set pieces: 'ao_yukawoyaburu' (bursting through the floor), 'ao_nawakui' (eating the rope ladder), 'ao_mushi4' (a creature variant), a door with an animated shadow ('kage') and a blood-splattered door swap ('_blood').
- Full-screen pictures do the heavy lifting for clues and scares: the memo, the map, the paint-caked and wiped piano, diary pages, the abacus cipher, the puppet and jewels, the closet doors sliding apart before a death, and 'semaruao', a three-frame zoom of the oni's face rushing the camera (the oikakekko back attack). Jump scares are rare and mostly sprite-driven: a sprite appearing in a doorway plus the music sting.
- Characters have face portraits in the message box (\f[hirosif] and similar) and short, flat dialogue lines.
- Sound is information: door SE = the oni entering or leaving; closet/fusuma slide SE = an ambush spawn; impact/cannon SE = a crash entrance; creature-breathing BGS = it is in the room while you hide. Rain and fire BGS appear only in specific scenes.
- Silence plus a single chase theme. No map autoplays BGM; 'horor-b' is the only gameplay music, and the endings use one separate track. The 2024 remaster adds 10 languages but keeps the look (per search extracts).

### Criticisms and pitfalls

- One-hit deaths plus frequent chases become repetitive trial-and-error (MobyGames player review). Reviewers of the 2025 sequel complain the random oni appears so often they must 'save every time they do a menial task' and forget what they were doing. Ration ambushes: the original's 5-minute cooldown is the fix, and the cooldown should be tuned to puzzle density.
- Obscure solutions: one critic says some puzzles need thinking 'so far outside the box that it's practically not in your view', and players reach for the wiki. Exact-tile-plus-facing item use risks pixel-hunting. Always pair the target spot with an examine line that hints at the verb.
- Hidden rules feel unfair when lethal. Examples: the closet's 10% random death even when you hid correctly, the invisible timer, death if you stand on the door tile when it arrives, and the stair kill box that catches players 'only slightly in contact' (reviewer complaint). Keep at least one telegraph for every lethal rule, such as door sound before entry or breathing before discovery.
- Scripted unavoidable deaths (taking the cell's star without padlocking; using the rope ladder before gathering everyone; trying the bathroom door 4 times) teach by killing. They are fine once or twice if the save point is close and the lesson is clear, and poisonous if frequent.
- The greedy, pathless AI gets stuck on furniture. The original patched specific rooms with hand-coded direction overrides. An original game should either use real pathfinding or deliberately design 'safe loops' as a readable mechanic.
- Thin story and plain RTP visuals are the most common critique (Indie Statik via search: solid puzzles and scares, weak graphics and story). The game survives on systems; a modern homage needs stronger writing to stand out.
- Version sprawl: 1.0 through 6.23 differ drastically in map, puzzles and plot, so walkthroughs disagree. If you ship updates, keep puzzle solutions stable or version your hints.
- Avoid copying iconic specifics. The blue giant with a human face, wardrobe-hiding with the 'two door sounds' rule, the piano/handkerchief code and the abacus cipher are all strongly associated with Ao Oni. Reuse the verbs (lure, overlay, heat-to-reveal, chair-under-glint) with new objects and fiction.

### Playtime and scope

["Playtime: reviews and forum posts (via search extracts) put a first run at roughly 1 hour. GameFAQs' crowd answer is about 3 hours (25 users), likely including deaths and retries. ## Mad Father (sen / Miscreant's Room, 2012, WOLF RPG Editor; 2016 Steam release; 2020 remake): mechanics, puzzle catalog, traps, chases, twist, endings, side characters, and why it works

Note on method: in this environment WebFetch failed with DNS errors on every domain, and the proxy blocked curl. Every fact below comes from WebSearch's extracts of the listed pages (more than 40 queries), not from reading the full pages. Mad Father is a short exploration-and-puzzle horror adventure. sen built it in WOLF RPG Editor, released it free on 10 Dec 2012, put it on Steam through Playism in 2016, and remade it for Switch/Steam on 5 Nov 2020. In it, 11-year-old Aya Drevis searches her mansion and the three basement floors below it to rescue her father. He is a doctor who turns people into dolls, and his victims have risen under a curse from her dead mother. The game runs on examining things and using items. Save points are frequent (crows). Danger comes in a few telegraphed bursts: stealth past dolls with a line of sight, mash or button-press escapes, and a chainsaw chase near the end. Many puzzles also tell the story, for example re-enacting a diary's memories so a blue flame goes out. The emotional hook is that the player is working to save a monster the protagonist loves. The true ending depends on an optional act of empathy (finding and reading the maid's diary), and the epilogue shows that the girl inherited her father's craft. Critics praise the atmosphere, sound, puzzles and morally grey cast. They fault obscure or missable steps, frustrating QTEs, and stilted dialogue.


### Mechanics

- **Examine-everything item adventure with readable affordances**: Aya walks a 3/4-view map and presses Z/Enter/Space to examine things. Items go into an inventory opened with Esc, which is split into regular items and key items. An item's name shows in white when it can be used now; gems also show white but can't be used. In the 2016 version a '!' appears over Aya's head when she stands near something interactable. The 2020 remake adds a 'Mission' menu that states the current goal, plus tips and mission markers. *Why it works:* Reviewers say the puzzles make you think without pixel-hunting (Gaming Hell). The '!' prompt and the white 'usable' text cut down on blind trying of every item on everything, so the challenge is the logic, not the search. The objective menu stops players from stalling during long backtracking.
- **Crow save points + generous retry**: You save by talking to crows perched around the house. Guides say you almost never lose access to a save point and one is nearly always within safe reach. The hardest chase has a save point right before it, and the stealth section and final chase also have checkpoints. In the remake, after a game over you continue from just before your death, no matter where you last saved (Famitsu). *Why it works:* Deaths are frequent, sometimes 'silly' and often instant, so cheap retries make them part of the content rather than a punishment. The Game Hoard: 'Save points are abundant to let the game get away with some of its more death-happy moments.' The diegetic crow fits the gothic mood.
- **Health bar that resets per area, plus instant-kill tier**: Weak monsters lower a health bar, and strong ones kill instantly. The bar refills from area to area (JayIsGames). Some zombies, such as those in the cafeteria, don't chase you but hurt you on contact. In the rat-infested room the rats drain health over time, so you have to grab the key and get out quickly. *Why it works:* There are two clear threat tiers, chip damage and instant death, and the player learns them fast. Refilling per area means you never start a room already doomed. The rat room turns health into a timer, which creates a dash without needing a chaser.
- **Grab-escape QTEs with a dedicated audio cue**: In the original, when a monster grabs Aya a specific song plays and the screen shows a finger, the Z key and 'Push!'. You mash Z/Space/Enter to fill a bar before her health runs out; failing gives 'YOU DIED'. The remake replaces mashing with on-screen directional inputs you copy, and some failures are instant deaths (Famitsu; TV Tropes 'Press X to Not Die'). *Why it works:* A fixed jingle and icon mean the player always knows what kind of danger this is, so the scare comes from the grab itself, not from confusing controls. The physical button-mashing mirrors Aya struggling. Reviewers still found the evasion and QTE parts the most frustrating; one spent 30+ minutes on a segment that should take about a minute.
- **Line-of-sight stealth with readable cover**: In the doll tunnel (B2F/B3F), knife-carrying dolls kill you the moment they see you. Cover includes rocks, raised ledges, holes in the walls you can duck into, and standing directly behind a doll. One doll rotates in a circle on a set pattern. Once Aya picks up the jail key the dolls die, so going back through is safe. *Why it works:* Facing direction is easy to read on 4-direction sprites, so sight lines can be learned and the player feels clever for escaping. Clearing the tunnel after the goal is met rewards success and removes tedious re-runs.
- **Utility tools instead of weapons**: The Mini Chainsaw from the attic can't hurt enemies. It breaks barrels, crates and boarded walls, cuts raw ham, beheads dolls in puzzles, cuts the cord holding a chandelier, and cuts overhead lights so they fall on a monster. The Empty Lighter, once filled with oil, burns the pile of dolls blocking the basement and burns a monster's flesh after Aya throws oil on it. *Why it works:* Aya stays a vulnerable child: she wins by solving the environment, not by fighting. One tool with many contextual uses keeps the inventory small and makes 'try the chainsaw on it' an expressive verb. The joke that a little girl's key tool is a chainsaw also foreshadows her father's weapon and what she inherits.
- **Small companion as a puzzle key**: Aya's pet rabbit Snowball is sent through a crack in the outside storehouse wall (reached through a door under the kitchen rug) to fetch Lighter Oil in the original, or wire in the remake. Snowball also spills wine in the storehouse, which reads as blood. *Why it works:* A second, smaller body solves 'too small for Aya' puzzles. Putting something cute and loved at risk in a horror space adds dread without any combat.
- **Traps you turn on enemies**: B3F: bait a monster into a room, pull a pot behind you to block it, then run out and slam its cell door shut. Tunnels: move a plank, weigh it down with sandbags, cross, and cut the chandelier cord when the patrolling enemy is one step from directly underneath. Lights get chainsawed onto a monster the same way. Final chase: dodge the father until he revs the chainsaw, which makes him stand still, then run past him and hit the switch. *Why it works:* These are puzzles with a timing element, not reflex combat. They reuse objects already taught (pushing pots, the chainsaw). A clear 'he stops when revving' rule turns a hard chase into one you can solve.
- **State items that cancel hazards**: With Mom's Perfume on, Aya can cross the rat hallway. A ghost girl, Ines, steals the perfume and runs. You get it back on B3F by unlocking a room, pushing a dresser aside to reveal a boarded-up wall, and chainsawing through it. *Why it works:* A hazard that wears down health becomes a lock, and a character-driven theft turns its key into a small story beat.
- **Optional ghost side quests that award collectible gems**: There are 21 gems in the original and 20 in the remake. Many come from helping friendly victims: put the Headless Man's head back on his body, or take Collina's dropped pendant to her Mourning Mother at the bedroom window or the pool of blood. The wiki counts about 10 non-hostile subjects. Collecting every gem unlocks a post-credits bonus scene and, in later versions, a Gallery/extra content; a '???' room on 2F also opens. *Why it works:* The victims become people, not just monsters, which feeds the moral theme. Thorough players get small reunions, and gems give replay value. Critics noted the helping doesn't actually change Aya's motivation.
- **Missable empathy flag decides the true ending**: When Maria collapses in the lab, you have to examine her twice to take her room key. That key opens the '???' door in the East Hall, where Maria's diary sits on a bookshelf. Reading it, then picking 'Save Father' in the other world, then using bandages from a bookshelf on Maria after Alfred attacks her, leads to the true ending. Without the diary you are forced into a bad ending. *Why it works:* The ending depends on understanding a character, not on stats. The weakness is that the key is easy to miss, and Gaming Hell calls it the most obtuse part of the game. Hint at it more clearly than the original does.
- **Recontextualizing replay**: Playing again after full completion adds new documents: a diary beside the safe in Mom's room, a scrap of paper in the Open Room, a diary on the table where Aya wakes up, and a letter from Mom to Dad on the lab floor. The remake adds Blood Mode, a second run as adult Aya with changed puzzles (she can do things child Aya can't, and the reverse), a changed story and a new ending. It unlocks after the true ending. *Why it works:* A short game gets a second meaning. Players already know the map, so new text alone can make familiar rooms unsettling.

### Puzzle examples

- (Mad Father) Counting-code safe: a Code Memo found in the Archives (in the 'Data of live experimentation' file) gives one clue per digit: count barrels in a 1F hall, chandeliers, red dolls in the Doll Room, and paintings. Two of the red dolls are hidden behind a curtain you have to pull aside, and one digit only comes from a drawer you climb up to open. The answer opens a safe in the 2F East bedroom that holds the Lamp (and, per one guide, the perfume). Guides disagree on the code (7482 vs 4280), which reflects version differences. *Lesson:* An observation puzzle sends players back through rooms they already know, so they look at them in a new way. Hiding one value behind something you have to interact with (the curtain) rewards thoroughness. Counting lots of small objects is fragile, so a later layout change can break the answer: keep the counts small and the objects distinctive.
- (Mad Father) Fuel chain: Lamp from the safe → door under the Kitchen rug → outside storehouse → send Snowball through a crack in the wall for Lighter Oil → fill the Empty Lighter → burn the pile of dolls blocking the basement passage and burn a monster's exposed flesh after splashing oil on it. *Lesson:* A 4–5 link dependency chain where each link is a different verb: open, go through, use the companion, combine, apply. Once the fire tool is gained, every flammable blockage in the game reads as a lock.
- (Mad Father) Trick Room (B2F): Ogre tells Aya to 'make the two sides match' (vgperson: 'not mirrored'). Steps: flip the right-hand painting, move a spear from one soldier figure to the armor, push the armor one tile left, take half the flowers from the left vase into the right one, and bring a 'king' figure down from the upper room and behead it with the chainsaw. In the 2016 version a dead girl on an altar sits up in the middle of her funeral once the room is solved. *Lesson:* A spot-the-difference room states its goal in one line and then uses every verb the player has learned (examine, take, push, place, cut). The scare goes on the solve itself, so relief becomes a scare.
- (Mad Father) Room of Memories / four diary rooms (B2F): each room shows a diary entry on the right and a blue flame blocking the way. You put the scene into the room to put the flame out: (1) push the baby sculpture to its mother; (2) put two animals in the dresser and push the mother out the door; (3) give the boy a kitchen knife and wipe the mother's face with the Napkin; (4) behead the girl figure with the chainsaw. Then examine the book at the top to go on. In the original, a book teleports to block the exit after you read a memory. *Lesson:* The puzzle is the storytelling. The clue text is both the instructions and the backstory, and the escalation from tender to violent tells a family history without cutscenes. An original version could use 3–4 rooms re-enacting your own villain's memories.
- (Mad Father) Hungry Boy (an item chain across areas): a ghost boy at the dining table says he's hungry → chainsaw a piece of raw ham in the cafeteria (now full of zombies that hurt on touch) → cook it on the kitchen stove → give him the cooked or plated ham → he hands over a Napkin → the Napkin is used in a Room of Memories scene. *Lesson:* A small fetch quest becomes meaningful when its reward unlocks a story puzzle somewhere else. Putting a hazard on the route back (zombies in the cafeteria) gives the backtracking some tension.
- (Mad Father) Toilet key (B2F): get Forceps from a crate in the B2F Laboratory, fish the Cell/Rusty Key out of a toilet, then wash it in the sink or bucket. In the remake Aya won't leave the room until it's cleaned. *Lesson:* A gross-but-logical lock fits the setting. The character's refusal ('she won't leave with it') guides the player in her own voice instead of a 'you can't do that' message.
- (Mad Father) Chandelier drop (Tunnels): climb a ladder while the patrolling enemy looks away, move a plank, weigh it with sandbags, cross, and cut the chandelier cord with the chainsaw when the enemy is a step from directly underneath. Reaching for the chandelier without the sandbags makes Aya fall to her death. Dropping it opens the way to the Cultivation Room. *Lesson:* Physics logic plus a deadly wrong answer teaches the rule ('the plank needs weight') through death. The solution also gives the player an indirect way to beat an enemy they can't fight.
- (Mad Father) Cultivation Room tile / slider puzzle: a picture puzzle is missing one piece, found later in the 'Dead End' area. Solving it opens a door. Players learned you could solve everything except the final tile, quit, then insert the piece. On repeat playthroughs it can be skipped. *Lesson:* A classic sliding puzzle is gated behind exploration (the missing piece). Letting players skip it on replays respects their time.
- (Mad Father) Monster trap (B3F): a monster lunges at the end of a hall. Keep the side cell open, duck inside, pull a pot left to block the tunnel, then run out and close the cell door on the monster. A later step moves the pot under a claw, hits a switch, and runs. *Lesson:* A chaser becomes a puzzle piece. Teach pushing and pulling pots early, so that under pressure the player only has to remember it.
- (Mad Father) Rat hallway + perfume: the rats drain health, so you grab a key from the rat room and leave fast. To go down the rat corridor safely you need Mom's Perfume, which Ines stole. It is recovered by pushing a dresser aside and chainsawing a boarded wall. *Lesson:* Turn an environmental hazard into a lock whose key is tied to a character (the dead mother's scent), and add a story beat (the theft) to the hunt for it.
- (Mad Father) Incinerator boss solve: instead of a fight, open the incinerator doors and drop the Creepy Doll into the fire. When it dies, every other animated doll dies too. *Lesson:* A 'boss' beaten by a puzzle action keeps the no-combat promise and clears a whole class of enemies at once, which is a satisfying pacing beat.
- (Mad Father) Final chainsaw chase: in the last room the father hunts Aya. Keep away until he revs the chainsaw; he then stands still, which gives you the window to run past and hit a switch in the upper-left. You do it again to get back to the door. There is a save point right before it. *Lesson:* Give the last chase a learnable rule announced by a sound (the rev). It feels terrifying but fair, and it is a skill test you can solve instead of pure reflex.
- (Mad Father) Reception Room / fireplace (1F): push a stool to a dresser and stand on it to reach the Cutting Knife. Carry a Wood Bucket of water from the courtyard to put out the fireplace. Move a painting aside, and drop an Eyeball Bottle down a hole in the fireplace. *Lesson:* Early 'teaching' puzzles use household logic (climb to reach, water beats fire) to set the vocabulary before the grotesque items show up.
- (Mad Father) Optional reunion quests: the Headless Man's head sits on a table in the B1F Mortuary and wants its body; reunite them for a gem. Collina grabs Aya in the Archives and shows her a vision of her death, then leaves a pendant; using it at the bedroom window (or the blood pool) reunites her with the Mourning Mother for a gem. *Lesson:* Optional side puzzles that give the victims some peace make them sympathetic and make the world feel populated. Gems give completionists a reason to explore.

### Atmosphere and pacing

- The game starts in domestic normalcy and drifts from it. Aya is a devoted daughter who hears animal and human screams from the basement and chooses not to know. The horror first gets into the house, the kitchen, cafeteria and bedrooms, before the player goes underground.
- The structure is a vertical descent: mansion 1F/2F (wings, attic, courtyard, storehouse) → B1 (Taxidermy Room, Laboratory, Cultivation Room, Mortuary) → B2 (tunnels, jail, Trick Room, Room of Memories) → B3 (rat hallway, cells, incinerator) → the shrine and the Other World portal → final chase. Each floor down is more grotesque and more dangerous.
- Rooms change when you come back. The first soul in the room next to Aya's is a jump scare. A bloated doll half-sunk in a pool of blood is fully out of the floor and facing the wall when you re-enter (the 2016 version swaps it for a ragdoll in a noose that appears only on first entry). After grabbing a gem from a floor crack under a vase, a hand reaches out once you walk away. After the wine-cellar errand the dinner room is overrun with corpses.
- Threat is rationed and back-loaded. Gaming Hell notes enemies are rare, and one-hit-kill chases mostly don't appear until near the end. They are telegraphed, unlike The Witch's House's unannounced chases. Most of the game is quiet exploration with sudden spikes.
- Death is treated as content. Guides say there are 'a lot of silly deaths' and 'you're not doing it wrong if you keep getting killed.' Wrong actions (reaching for the chandelier with no counterweight) are fatal, which teaches the player to read the environment.
- Pacing problems: early backtracking feels unmotivated until more of the map opens up, and then the game 'finds its stride' (Game Hoard). One gem needs a dog bone you only get later, which forces backtracking.
- Sound carries the dread: reviewers cite 'excellent sound design' that makes up for simple pixels, piano melodies mixed with droning, and a soundtrack called possibly the best among RPG Maker horror games. Audio cues double as mechanics (the QTE jingle, the chainsaw rev).

### Story structure

- Premise: 11-year-old Aya Drevis lives in a mansion in northern Germany with her father, a doctor (named 'Alfred' in fan translation; vgperson notes most characters are unnamed in the Japanese, so 'Alfred' was a placeholder name), and the maid/lab assistant Maria. Her mother Monika died a year earlier, officially of illness. The father experiments on people and turns them into dolls, helped by Maria.
- Inciting incident: on the anniversary of Monika's death, her curse brings the father's victims (and the dolls) back to life to take revenge on him, and he is taken. The player's goal is to rescue the villain. That reversal is the core of the game.
- Guide figure: Ogre, a polite, bald, red-eyed 'wandering salesman', first appears in Aya's bedroom. He explains the curse, points her in the right direction without solving things for her, sets puzzle trials (the Trick Room), and before the Other World gives her 'Magic Water', a new product from his company that can 'seal the curse'. He also helped Monika place the curse, so he is ambiguous, and he is heavily implied to be the same demon as in sen's earlier Misao.
- Side characters humanize the victims. About 10 subjects are friendly: Collina and the Mourning Mother (a reunion), the Headless Man (wants his body), the Hungry Boy (trades a napkin for food), Ines (steals the perfume), and Robin, a one-eyed blond boy whose first appearance frightens Aya and who later covers the socket with bandages. Snowball the rabbit is Aya's one ally from her normal life.
- The truth comes out gradually through diaries, letters, flashbacks (the father injecting 'medicine') and the re-enactments in the Room of Memories. The father notices Aya hiding animal corpses and sees himself in her ('exactly as I used to').
- Twist: Monika found out the father planned to turn Aya into a doll. She tried to leave with Aya, and he murdered her; the death was passed off as illness (fan wikis differ on the exact method). The curse is mostly read as Monika protecting Aya, though some read it as possessiveness. Some wiki readings say Monika wanted Aya as heir to her own family's dark tendencies.
- Climactic choice at the portal: 'Save Father' with the Magic Water, or grant Mother's wish and let her drag him away. Bad Ending 1 (grant the wish): Aya offers the shocked Maria a home, and Maria, still loyal, knocks her out and makes her a doll as the father's last request. Bad Ending 2 (save him without having read Maria's diary): Aya learns he cheated on Monika and wants her as a doll. During the chase he wounds Maria's leg with the chainsaw, and Aya trips over a doll at the shrine and is killed.
- True Ending (diary read + Save Father + bandage Maria): Monika's ghost shows Aya how she died. The father betrays Maria, chainsawing her leg and calling her useless. Aya bandages her, Maria throws knives at him to save Aya, and the two agree to live together. Epilogue: grown-up Aya runs a free clinic in the woods and turns her patients into dolls, but anesthetizes them first and treats them as people, not parts. All gems unlock a post-credits scene of the father with an Aya clone in another world.
- Remake additions: a cutscene about Robin's origin; Ogre is shown taking the father to his realm. In Blood Mode, adult Aya, trapped in a nightmare version of the house, confronts the ghost of a girl she killed. It confirms she kills terminally ill patients 'mercifully', and it ends with her hugging her chainsaw-wielding father and pouring Magic Water on him.
- Why it lands: (1) Love for someone who doesn't deserve it, since the child's devotion makes every reveal hurt. (2) No clean hands: father, maid, mother and Aya are all compromised, as reviewers note. (3) Theme of inheritance ('it runs in the family'), with a happy ending that is also unsettling. (4) The best ending is unlocked by reading the antagonist's diary, so the player's own empathy is the key. (5) Optional reunions give the victims dignity. Critics say it 'waffles' on how evil Aya is and that helping victims doesn't change her motivation.

### Art and audio

- Engine and look: WOLF RPG Editor, a 3/4 top-down view with 4-direction sprites in a 16-bit style that reviewers call 'very well done and detailed', plus bust portraits beside dialogue. Glitchwave tags it 'graphic adventure, J-horror, 3/4 view, pixel art'.
- Color and effects: reviewers point to a crimson palette and to 'clever use of effects and filters' (screen tints, darkness, flashes) that make up for simple sprites. The remake adds an optional noise filter.
- Gore is shown with small, readable cues: a pool of blood under a sprite, a doll half-sunk in the floor, a head on a table, a sheet over a tank. The taxidermy room, the doll room and the incinerator carry the 'dolls made of people' imagery.
- Portraits show character and change between versions. In the original the father's face is hidden behind a mask in early portraits, and the remake shows it. Aya's and the mother's portraits were redone in version 2.0, and earlier versions had very different Aya art. The remake makes sprites bigger so clothing and small gestures read better, details the monsters more and adds more zombie variety, though some players preferred the original pixels.
- Music: lots of piano, both beautiful and creepy, plus droning ambience. One reviewer called it possibly the best soundtrack in an RPG Maker horror game.
- Sound as gameplay: grab events play their own song with a 'Push!' finger icon, and the father's chainsaw rev is the cue for the final chase. One reviewer complained the SFX are too loud and can't be turned down, so give separate SFX and music volume controls.

### Criticisms and pitfalls

- Obscure puzzles: several reviewers (natalie.tf, Noisy Pixel) needed a walkthrough. Avoid solutions that depend on a counted value that's easy to miss, and give a recap or hint channel. The remake's Mission menu was added for this.
- Missable true-ending item: you have to examine Maria twice for her key, and the chance can be lost permanently. Gaming Hell calls it the most obtuse part of the game. Signal it clearly or make it hard to miss.
- QTE and evasion frustration: one reviewer spent 30+ minutes on a segment meant to take about a minute. Enemies can occasionally pin Aya against walls. Keep chases short, telegraphed, checkpointed and forgiving about collisions.
- Unmotivated early backtracking, and gem steps that need items you only get later (the stuffed dog's bone). Gate optional puzzles so the solution can be found when the puzzle is first seen, or mark it clearly as 'come back later'.
- Some reviewers felt it leaned on jump scares and instant deaths, and that the mansion setting dulled tension. natalie.tf later called it more 'a showcase of cool concepts' than true horror.
- Stilted dialogue (possibly from translation) is a recurring complaint, so write natural, sparse lines.
- Story critiques: it waffles on how evil Aya is, and helping the victims doesn't change her motivation. Give your optional kindness some story consequence.
- Technical problems: the 2016 PC build had save and load bugs, and the remake had occasional bugs that forced restarts. Test save/load thoroughly.
- Version drift: walkthroughs give different safe codes (7482 vs 4280) and different item names between versions. Keep puzzle data in one source of truth so clues and answers can't drift apart.
- Since Blood Mode, some players find the ending's ambiguity spoiled. Over-explaining in follow-up content can weaken an ending that works by unsettling the player.

### Playtime and scope

Measured length: HowLongToBeat (via Dekudeals/GG.deals) gives about 3h for the main story, 4h main + extras, 5h completionist. Famitsu puts a first run of the remake at about 2.5–3h. Hardcore Gamer took about 4h to get every gem and replay the finale for each ending. Uppercut said about 6h. Scope of the original: one mansion (two floors, attic, courtyard, storehouse), three basement levels, a shrine, and an Other World finale. 21 gems (20 in the remake). Three endings plus a bonus scene; Hardcore Gamer counts 5 ending variants. A core cast of 5 (Aya, father, Maria, Monika's ghost, Ogre) plus about 10 friendly ghosts and a rabbit companion. Roughly 15–20 main puzzles plus optional gem puzzles, one stealth gauntlet, a few QTE grabs and 2–3 chases. For a 1–2 hour original game, scale to about 60% of this: for example a house plus 2 underground layers, 12–16 required puzzles, 4–6 optional ghost side puzzles, one stealth section, 2 chases (the last one telegraphed, with a save right before), and 3 endings with an empathy-based flag for the true ending.

## Ib (kouri, 2012, RPG Maker 2000; 2022 RPG Maker MV remake published by Playism): cursed art gallery, rose-as-health, companions Garry and Mary, puzzles, scares, endings driven by hidden counters, atmosphere, and why players love it

Ib is a short, combat-free, top-down (3/4 view) horror adventure. A 9-year-old girl visiting a gallery of works by the fictional artist Guertena is pulled into a living, cursed version of the exhibition. Her health is a rose: 5 petals for Ib and 10 for Garry. Petals fall from enemy contact, hazards and dialogue, and only vases restore them. Her companions are Garry, an adult, and Mary, a girl who turns out to be Guertena's last painting. The game tracks small, innocent-looking interactions in hidden counters: Garry "bond", Garry "doom" and Mary "affection". These decide 7 to 10 endings, depending on how they are counted. Critics praise its atmosphere, its silence, its surreal art-comes-alive premise and its emotional companion beats more than its puzzle difficulty. ### Mechanics

- **Rose as health bar (a physical, stealable life)**: Each human character carries a rose whose petals are their HP. Ib's red rose holds up to 5 petals but has only 3 when she first picks it up. Garry's blue rose has 10 because he is an adult. A UI window shows the rose and its petal count. Petals are lost through direct contact with enemies, environmental hazards, and scripted dialogue or events. When the controlled character's rose runs out of petals, they die instantly and the game is over. Mary carries a yellow rose that turns out to be fake: it is not her health, and she uses it to pass as human. *Why it works:* The health bar is an object inside the fiction, so it can drive the plot. Garry is introduced writhing on the floor because the Lady in Blue is plucking petals from his stolen rose. Ib must retrieve the rose, heal it in a vase and hand it back before he joins her. Late in the game Mary bargains with roses. A diegetic, fragile life meter also feels more vulnerable than a number.
- **Vases as healing points (scarce, plus a trap)**: Placing a rose in a vase is the only way to restore petals. Most vases work once and then stay dry for the rest of the game. A light-blue vase has unlimited water, and reviewers note that a vase sprite drawn full and blue generally means unlimited uses. The Red Area's central vase is infinitely reusable. One vase holds red paint instead of water and withers the rose if Ib uses it. *Why it works:* It is resource tension without combat or an inventory economy. Players learn to read sprite art as information (full and blue means safe). The poisoned vase punishes autopilot and teaches that even safe things in this world can betray you.
- **No combat: evasion and chase sequences**: The player cannot fight enemies, only escape them. The Lady in Red tears herself out of her frame and crawls after Ib. Ib has to lure her away from the Red Key she drops, then double back for it. The Lady in Blue pounds on a window and then smashes through it. She stops chasing once Ib re-enters the central room. Headless statues either stand as static blockers or come alive and try to pin Ib against walls. *Why it works:* Without combat the player stays powerless, the core feeling of horror. Chases are rare set pieces placed between quieter puzzle rooms, so each one spikes the tension. JayIsGames called the chase sequences the only real difficulty.
- **Companion party with point-of-view switching**: Garry joins after Ib saves his rose, and Mary joins later. In the Brown Area a wall of stone vines separates Garry from Ib and Mary. In this 'separation puzzle' the player controls both sides. Stepping on a blue-rose floor image switches control to Garry, and a red-rose image switches back to Ib. Actions on one side open the way for the other. In the remake a dedicated button lets Ib talk to Garry or Mary, who give hints and add new dialogue. *Why it works:* Companions break the isolation, which makes losing them hurt. Splitting the party creates two-sided cooperative puzzles. It also lets the camera show the player things Ib cannot see, such as Garry learning the truth about Mary.
- **Hidden relationship counters (Garry bond, Garry doom, Mary affection)**: Moments that do not look like choices are tracked silently. Garry DOOM goes up when he kicks the mannequin head in the mirror room, when the wrong (rightmost) cord is pulled in the cord room, when he kicks the persistent blue doll, and when he fails the doll room (+2). It also rises if Ib tells Mary she would rather leave with Garry, or if all three mannequin heads are broken when only one is needed. At doom 3 or more, Garry dies in the toy box. Garry BOND goes up when Ib reads the poster in the maze room, stops Garry from kicking the mannequin head, or picks up his coat in the safe room. Talking to him enough times in the safe room and in the 'fake sunlight' room also raises it. At bond 8 or more (one guide says 9 or more, with doom under 3), Promise of Reunion is possible. Mary AFFECTION goes up when Ib looks at the 'sinister painting' of herself collapsed in the hide-and-seek room, agrees that Red Eyes is cute, or agrees that she and Mary will be fine on their own. One mirror of the guide adds talking to Mary 7 or more times in the Brown Area. At affection 4 or more, Mary's ending is possible. *Why it works:* The endings come from how you treated people, not from one big final choice. Kicking an object for fun has consequences hours later, and that feels earned on a second playthrough. The downside is that the system is opaque, and most players use guides to find the good endings.
- **Fail-forward timed events**: In the doll room, Garry picks up a ball of paint and the door locks behind him. Blue glow and bell chimes start as Red Eyes rises out of its frame. The key is hidden inside one of many blue dolls, and its position is randomized across about four layouts. If Garry kicked the doll earlier, he gets less time. If he moved it aside instead, he gets more. Failing is not a game over. The camera cuts away as Red Eyes reaches for Garry, he is later found traumatized and unable to remember, and doom goes up by 2. *Why it works:* Failure turns into story and dread instead of a reload. Not showing what happened is scarier than showing it. Linking the timer to an earlier careless act makes cause and effect feel personal.
- **Paintings and sculptures as interactive puzzle objects**: The art in the cursed gallery is a set of tools and threats. Ib can take the Ant Painting off its wall and lay it over a hole as a bridge. Paintings can hold clues, block doors, or be burned with Garry's lighter (which also burns thorns). Two 'informant' paintings, Strained Ear and Tattletale, relay Garry's discovery from one floor to Mary on another. *Why it works:* One art language serves as scenery, enemy, tool and narrator. Players learn to inspect everything, and every inspected frame might come alive. That is how the game keeps one premise fresh for its whole length.
- **Diegetic hints and information carried across rooms**: Clues are written on walls, given by talking portraits, or taught by the normal gallery in the prologue. A wall in the Violet Area asks for the largest painting in the Guertena Art Gallery. The answer is 'Abyss of the Deep', which the player could have seen during the prologue, and it later opens a door. The Yellow Area's door code 166 combines three coloured numbers hidden around the area (18 x 9 + 4). *Why it works:* Paying attention during the calm prologue pays off later. Puzzles stay rooted in the world instead of in abstract UI. Gathering scattered pieces of a code builds a light exploration loop.
- **New Game+ gated ending and memory framing**: 'Welcome to the World of Guertena' cannot be reached on a first playthrough. Before the final exit, the Fabricated World painting warns that there is no turning back and that Ib's memories of the adventure will be forgotten. Endings then depend on a keepsake: the handkerchief Ib gave Garry when he cut his hand. *Why it works:* The forgetting mechanic turns a plain escape into something bittersweet. A small physical token becomes the emotional proof of the relationship, and gating one ending behind a replay rewards players who return.
- **Remake additions: Zoom and Conversation**: In an Automaton interview, kouri said the 2022 remake added two systems, 'Zoom' and 'Conversation', in which companions give hints and chat. It also added on-screen control guidance. Some unclear event messages from the original, such as 'something occurred outside the room', were made clearer or rebuilt as new puzzles. The story was deliberately left unchanged. The remake also adds bonus areas, including a 'True Guertena Exhibit' and the Dungeon, Orange, Cyan/Abyssal Space and Black areas. *Why it works:* These fix the original's main usability problems (vague feedback, no hints, no explanation of controls) without changing the tone. A hint-giving companion keeps a player who is stuck in the fiction instead of sending them to a walkthrough.

### Puzzle examples

- (Ib) Ant Painting bridge and statue trap (Green Area). Ib inspects the Ant Painting twice to take it off the wall, then lays it over a large hole in another room as a bridge. Beyond it, she picks up the Green Key, and a headless statue in the room comes to life and chases her. When she runs back across the painting bridge, it tears, and the statue falls into the new hole and is destroyed. The Green Key opens the door next to where the Ant Painting used to hang. *Lesson:* One object does three jobs: it is a tool (bridge), a scripted trap that rescues you, and a reveal (the door was hidden behind the painting). The chase resolves through something the player built, so escaping feels clever, not lucky.
- (Ib) Lady in Red chase (Red Area). A normal-looking portrait in the upper-right corner breaks out of its frame when inspected and chases Ib. The Red Key appears on the floor at the same moment. The player has to lure her away, loop around and grab the key, then unlock the central door, which leads to a library where pushing or reading a book on the top-left shelf opens the next door. *Lesson:* The thing you investigate releases both the threat and the reward together, so getting the reward means pulling the threat away from it. That is a basic chase-puzzle hybrid.
- (Ib) Saving Garry (Red Area). Garry is found on the floor in pain because the Lady in Blue is plucking petals from his blue rose. Talking to him gets the Small Key. Ib dodges the Lady in Blue and takes the dropped Blue Rose, escapes while she pounds on and then breaks through a window, and is safe once back in the central room. She heals the blue rose in the infinite vase and returns it, and Garry joins and moves a heavy headless statue that Ib could not move alone. *Lesson:* The companion is introduced through the same health mechanic the player already understands, so the player rescues him with their own tools. The companion's first act opens a path the child could not open, which establishes why the companion matters.
- (Ib) Liars' Room (Yellow Area). Several portraits each make claims about who tells the truth. Red and Yellow both say White is honest, and since the three agree, all are liars. Blue says Green is the only truthful one, which undercuts itself, so Blue lies too. Brown is the only one nobody vouches for, and he is the truthful one: 'stand in front of the statue, go 4 steps east and 2 north'. A loose tile there shows a coloured '4', one of the three digits for the door code 18 x 9 + 4 = 166. Afterwards the other liars kill Brown. *Lesson:* A classic logic riddle whose answer is a physical place, not a password, so the solution is walking. A short violent payoff afterwards turns a puzzle room into a horror beat.
- (Ib) Gallery trivia password (Violet Area). Writing on the wall asks for the name of the largest painting in the real Guertena gallery, three words long. The answer is 'Abyss of the Deep', which the player could see in the safe prologue gallery. Using it on the upper door at the end of the hall opens it. *Lesson:* Reward players for paying attention during the calm opening. A slow, normal-world tutorial can secretly plant answers for later. Giving the word count allows process-of-elimination as a fallback.
- (Ib) Gray Area eyedrops and congested eye. A room full of movable stools and fixed easels forms a push-block maze with eyedrops at the end, and a small painting that falls gives a hint. Using the eyedrops on a red, congested eye among the eyes on the floor makes it reveal a secret passage. *Lesson:* An ordinary Sokoban maze is reskinned with body horror. The object you get is a 'cure' that you apply to the monster, which gives a strange feeling of tenderness toward the world instead of violence.
- (Ib) Mirror room mannequin head (Gray Area). A mannequin head blocks the exit. Look at the mirror a second time and the head appears over Garry's shoulder in the reflection. Garry moves to kick it, and Ib can stop him or cover her eyes. Letting him kick it adds +1 doom, and the mirror later shows both their eyes scratched out with 'EYE' scrawled across it. A mannequin head also appears hanging above a vase in the next room. *Lesson:* A morality test disguised as a simple obstacle. The consequence shows up in the environment right away and also lands in an ending far later. Small acts of violence by the companion are tracked.
- (Ib) Doll room (Violet Area, Garry alone). Garry takes a ball of paint and the door locks. Blue light and bell chimes start while Red Eyes rises from its frame and the dolls turn their heads toward the camera. The exit key is inside one randomized blue doll, and the time limit depends on whether Garry kicked or moved the blue doll that kept appearing ahead of him earlier. Failing cuts away and adds +2 doom instead of ending the game. *Lesson:* A timed search with a random solution under rising audiovisual pressure, linked to an earlier ethical choice. Failure feeds the story. The This Cage Is Worms essay notes the earlier doll keeps reappearing ahead of you and reacts with hurt when spoken to, which plays on the player's guilt.
- (Ib) Cord room separation puzzle (Brown Area). Vines split Garry from Ib and Mary. Ib steps on a blue-rose floor tile to switch control to Garry, who finds a bookcase that opens a hole into a room with five cords and a red rose tile. The second cord from the right moves the painting 'Bashful Glance' over a hole on Ib's side, so she can cross. Ib then pushes a triangular box down the hole, it lands in the cord room, and Garry pushes it into a hole to open his door. Wrong cords gas Garry, plunge the room into darkness, or add doom. *Lesson:* A two-character cooperative puzzle where each side's action unlocks the other, using rose-colour tiles as the control-switch language. Wrong answers hurt the character you care about, not 'you', so trial and error feels guilty.
- (Ib) Sketchbook switch room and wilted tulip (Mary's crayon world). Eight switches surround a centre and must be pressed in a set order (N, W, NE, NW, SE, S, E, SW). Every mistake costs a petal and resets the sequence. Elsewhere, Ib fills a bucket from a pond to make a wilted crayon tulip bloom and gets the Gallery Key, which is itself drawn in crayon. *Lesson:* Sequence puzzles become tense when each mistake costs health. A whole area drawn in a child's crayon style, including the items, shows the antagonist's mind through art direction.
- (Ib) Violet Area snake and maze. Look at the belly of a snake artwork to get a glass heart, then give it to an artwork with hands sticking out. A coloured-tile maze is solved by stepping on blue triangle, orange circle, pink square, then orange circle again. *Lesson:* An item handed from one artwork to another (body-part logic: a heart for the hands) reads as dream logic but stays solvable because both objects are in the same area.

### Atmosphere and pacing

- Calm prologue as a baseline. Ib arrives at the real gallery with her parents, they stop at reception, and she asks to go ahead alone. The player walks through a normal exhibition, reading painting names that come back later. Horror starts only when she looks at 'Fabricated World' (shown to her as '??? World'): the lights flicker and go out, red fluid leaks from a window, a man bangs on a window, and a message beginning 'COME IB…' appears.
- Horror through things coming alive, not gore. JayIsGames describes 'surreal scares and strangeness rather than gore'. Glitchwave says it has few gimmicky deaths and jump scares compared with similar RPG Maker horror games. The This Cage Is Worms essay argues it works through transformation: static art suddenly moves at unpredictable times, which makes up for RPG Maker having no first-person view and only basic audio.
- A catalogue of uncanny moments to learn from (not copy). Women in portraits crawl like spiders. Headless statues move only when you are not watching and pin you against walls. A giant mannequin head drifts toward you. Eyes cover the floor, one of them bloodshot, and they close when you leave. Claws reach from wall edges. A giant red hand blocks a painting. Dolls turn their heads in unison to look at the camera. A mirror shows your eyes scratched out with 'EYE' scrawled across it.
- Silence and ominous music. TV Tropes notes each area has a small set of uniformly ominous BGM tracks that hint something bad is about to happen. A review praises the 'masterful use of silence' that makes sounds land harder.
- Colour-coded areas, each themed by floor colour (Blue, Green, Yellow, Red, Gray, Violet, Brown, then Mary's crayon Sketchbook). This gives a sense of progress and a memorable identity to each chapter.
- Rhythm of tension and relief. Quiet exploration and puzzle rooms alternate with short chases. Safe rooms let Ib talk to Garry, which is also where bond is earned, and a 'fake sunlight' room offers a false sense of comfort.
- Dramatic irony through point-of-view switching. While separated, the player follows Garry into a small library where he learns Mary is a painting, while Ib is still alone with her. The paintings 'Strained Ear' and 'Tattletale' carry the news to Mary, who freezes. The player knows the danger before Ib does.
- Constant low-level discomfort even in colourful places. A review says it feels 'creepy and off putting even in the most colorful scenarios', and Mary's crayon world uses a childlike art style to unsettling effect.

### Story structure

- Act 0, real world: a family trip to a gallery of works by the fictional artist Guertena. The prologue teaches movement and inspecting, and plants knowledge such as painting names.
- Act 1, alone: Ib is pulled into the cursed gallery, finds her rose (3 of 5 petals) and learns about vases. She gets through the first areas (Blue, Green, and per the wiki's order Yellow) using keys, painting puzzles and the first chase.
- Act 2, the protector: in the Red Area Ib finds Garry being 'killed' through his stolen rose. She saves him, he joins and moves heavy statues. The pair travel through the Gray Area (eyes, mirror, mannequins), where the hidden bond/doom tests start.
- Act 3, the third friend: Mary, a cheerful girl about Ib's age, joins (one walkthrough puts this in the Violet Area after a sculpture puzzle). Early clues hint she is not what she seems: she ignores Garry's advice about protecting her rose, and her yellow rose is fake.
- Act 4, separation and reveal: vines split the group. Garry solves puzzles alone (doll room, cord room) and discovers in a library that Mary is Guertena's last painting, who wants to escape into the real world. Informant paintings warn Mary, Garry rejoins, Mary turns hostile and is fought off.
- Act 5, the antagonist's world: the Sketchbook, Mary's crayon world, with a toy box and a 'white house'. Mary forces a rose bargain. If doom is 3 or more, Garry gives up his rose and dies there. Mary asks Ib who she would rather leave with. Mary's painting can be burned with Garry's lighter, which ends her (in one route she chases them with a knife until they reach it).
- Act 6, the threshold: the Fabricated World painting is the exit. It warns that memories will be lost. A Fake Mother appears (Garry cannot see her) and tells Ib not to follow strangers, while Garry urges her to jump. In the dark gallery route, a Fake Garry can lure her instead.
- Endings (7 to 10 depending on how variants are counted). PROMISE OF REUNION: Garry survives, bond is high enough, Ib gave him her handkerchief and took his hand. Back home they meet again, and the handkerchief becomes the token of a memory they half-recall. MEMORY'S CRANNIES: the same, but without the handkerchief, so both keep only faint, melancholy recollections. IB ALL ALONE: Ib follows the Fake Mother or Fake Garry. FORGOTTEN PORTRAIT: Garry died, Ib takes his lighter and burns Mary's painting, escapes, and finds a new painting of Garry sleeping in the real gallery (replacing a 'Hanged Man' piece) before her mother calls her away. TOGETHER, FOREVER: Garry died, Mary's painting was not burned, and Mary escapes and becomes Ib's 'sister', with the parents accepting her as their second child. WELCOME TO THE WORLD OF GUERTENA: only on a later playthrough, needing Mary affection 4 or more, doom 5 or more, seeing Ib's collapsed portrait in hide-and-seek, Garry failing the doll room, and telling Mary you would leave with her. Ib stays as a painting and Mary leaves. A PAINTING'S DEMISE: Mary's affection is too low or Ib picks Garry, and Mary is destroyed. Sources disagree on some exact thresholds.
- Themes: art imitating life, the loneliness of a creation that longs to be real, an adult protecting a child, and memory and forgetting. The game shows that the three main characters can never all be saved: at most two can escape.

### Art and audio

- RPG Maker 2000, 3/4 top-down view, small character sprites in tight corridor rooms. Each area has its own floor colour.
- A whole fictional artist's catalogue is used as level art. Named works include Fabricated World, Abyss of the Deep, The Lady in Red/Blue (plus green and yellow variants), Death of the Individual (headless statues), Red Eyes, Strained Ear, Tattletale, Breath, Soul-Sapping Throng, Bashful Glance, Grieving Bride (hands), the Ant Painting, a row of bug paintings including a Spider Painting, and a five-piece butterfly life-cycle series (Prologue, Chapter 1, Chapter 2, Last Chapter, Epilogue). Naming every artwork gives the world authorship and invites the player to inspect everything.
- Enemy design comes from art categories: framed portraits that crawl out, headless sculptures, mannequins and giant mannequin heads, blue dolls, eyes on the floor, wall claws. Most of them are static until they suddenly move.
- The antagonist's area uses a different visual style: crayon drawings, crayon-drawn keys and items. This shows the character's mind through art direction.
- Colour symbolism through the roses: red (Ib), blue (Garry), yellow (Mary, which is fake). The fan wiki reads them through flower language (yellow as friendship and betrayal), but that is fan interpretation.
- Audio: a few ominous BGM tracks per area, a lot of silence so that sound cues hit harder, and sound stings for chases (bell chimes and glow in the doll room). The remake replaced the sound effects and added new art and redesigned artworks.

### Criticisms and pitfalls

- Short. Some reviewers call it too short and say it does not go deep enough into its themes and artworks.
- Puzzles are 'not very complicated'. Most are key hunts, push-blocks, codes and inspections, and the difficulty comes from chases.
- Opaque ending requirements. Hidden bond/doom/affection counters tied to small actions mean most players need a guide (such as vgperson's) to see the good or secret endings. Even guides disagree on thresholds (bond 8 versus 9).
- Randomized key placement in the timed doll room can feel unfair, and failing changes the ending.
- Penalties for trial and error. The switch room takes a petal on every wrong press and resets, and wrong cords hurt Garry.
- Vague feedback in the original. Event messages like 'something occurred outside the room' were unclear, and there was no control guidance, which kouri fixed in the remake.
- Technical decay. The RPG Maker 2000 build stopped running on some modern hardware, which was a stated reason for the remake.
- Some players do not find it scary. A Steam discussion reports few jump scares, though others disagree, so the tone leans toward unease and melancholy rather than terror.
- Design pitfall for imitators: the game relies on a fragile companion bond. If the player does not care about the companion, the counters, the forgetting ending and the death endings lose their force, so give the companion plenty of small, optional moments.

### Playtime and scope

There are no published figures for the original free version. For the remake, HowLongToBeat (via PlatPrices) lists about 3 hours for the main story, about 4 hours with extras and about 7.5 hours for completionists. GG.deals estimates about 2h46m for the main story, and all trophies take about 4 hours. The scope is about 8 or more colour-themed areas (Blue, Green, Yellow, Red, Gray, Violet, Brown, the Sketchbook) and a final threshold. The sources disagree on exact order and on where Mary joins. The remake adds post-game bonus areas: the Dungeon, Orange, Cyan/Abyssal Space and Black areas, and the True Guertena Exhibit. There are 7 to 10 endings, one of them locked to New Game+, which encourages 2 to 3 playthroughs. Two playable companions plus about 3 hidden counters provide most of the branching. For a 1–2 hour original game, this suggests about 8–10 compact areas of about 8–12 minutes each. Each area should have one signature puzzle, one or two inspect, key or code beats, and one scare or chase, with safe rooms between acts for companion dialogue.

## The Witch's House (Fummy, 2012, RPG Maker VX; remade as The Witch's House MV in 2018): how its instant-death traps, cat save points, diary storytelling and twist ending work, and why they hold up as design

The Witch's House is a freeware horror adventure made by one Japanese developer, Fummy, in RPG Maker VX. It came out on 3 October 2012. Fummy has said Ib was an influence. A remake, The Witch's House MV, was published by DANGEN on 31 October 2018 for PC and reached consoles in 2022. The player controls Viola, a 13-year-old girl. She wakes in a forest where walls of roses block the way out, so she goes into her friend Ellen's house. The house has five floors and changes as you play. There is no combat and no HP: one wrong action kills Viola instantly. Traps are hinted at by blood smears, notes, odd room details and sounds. A talking black cat is the only save point, and it shows up often enough that dying costs little progress. Diary pages scattered through the house slowly tell Ellen's backstory: a sick, neglected girl who made a pact with a demon. In two endings the game reveals that the 'Viola' you played was Ellen, who had stolen Viola's body. The legless, eyeless 'witch' chasing you at the end is the real Viola, and in the ending her own father shoots her. ### Mechanics

- **One-hit instant-death traps (no HP, no combat)**: Reviewers say almost anything can kill you: picking up an item or leaving it, talking to something or ignoring it, or just being in the wrong place. Deaths recorded in the sources: (1) in the first room, stepping on a blood stain makes the walls slide shut and crush Viola; walking around the stain is safe. (2) Using the machete on the LARGE rose patch snaps the blade off into Viola's skull; it has to go on the small patch near the start. (3) Agreeing to read the Book of Death means game over; the safe choice is Cancel. (4) Other deaths listed by TV Tropes: walls that slide, a piano that eats you, a giant toy soldier that shoots you, a grandfather clock that falls on you. Each death has its own grisly cutscene, and fans have compiled them into 'all deaths' videos. *Why it works:* Every interaction feels like a gamble, so the game is tense without a monster hunting you. One reviewer put it this way: the dread comes from knowing your own actions push you deeper, not from a stalker like Resident Evil's Mr X. Each death plays like a shocking punchline, and that shock is part of the fun.
- **Telegraphing that keeps trial and error fair**: Reviewers note that smears of blood and memos with scrawled warnings hint at what lies ahead, so most deaths can be avoided after some trial and error. Chases are usually announced by a sound or by an object changing. Some clues are puzzle instructions written in the world: a note saying 'BEARS IN THE BASKET', or a wall note telling you to go where only one eye is open. Risky actions often come with an explicit yes/no prompt, such as whether to read the Book of Death. TV Tropes files the deaths under Developer's Foresight, because many are hidden in out-of-the-way places only curious players find. *Why it works:* The first death shocks you. On the retry you spot the clue that was there all along, so the death feels like your mistake rather than the game cheating. Instant death plus a clue you can find later turns punishment into learning.
- **Talking black cat as the only save point**: A black cat that talks appears at various places in the house. You talk to it, go through its dialogue, and then you can save. It is the only way to save. Reviewers say it appears often enough that a death doesn't cost much lost time. Right before the witch's room you find the cat's dead body, and it still works as a save point. The secret ending depends on never saving or never talking to the cat; sources disagree on the exact rule and say it varies by version. In that ending the cat turns out to be a demon. In the original version (not the MV), talking to the cat could also make the house's door open by itself. *Why it works:* The save point lives inside the story instead of being a menu option, so it can be part of the plot: its corpse is a shock, and its secret identity is a twist. Putting cats close together is what makes instant deaths bearable.
- **Difficulty modes (MV remake)**: On Easy, dying sends you back to the start of the room or the spot where you died. On Normal, you go back to the title screen and your last cat save. Extra unlocks after the true ending and deliberately breaks what veterans know. Watering the 1st-floor clock instead of the garden one drops you into a black void with no exit. The frog runs off into a hole you can't reach, so the snake has to be fed a stuffed frog instead. In the eye-wall puzzle every eye is open, and the answer is on the opposite wall, which the top-down view doesn't show. The red grasses' suggestions now lead to game over. Trap positions in the poison hallway are randomized. The timer scene is harder. Extra also adds new deaths and changed lines, plus scenes and lore about the witch. *Why it works:* Easy keeps instant deaths from frustrating new players. Extra turns the player's memory against them, so experienced players feel the original's unpredictability again, and it adds replay value.
- **Item-chain puzzles whose solutions are cruel**: Teddy bear chain: take the bear from the presents room (northeast, 1st floor). Its limbs make it too big for the basket, so cut them off with the chained scissors on the workbench. Put the torso in the basket under the 'BEARS IN THE BASKET' note, which opens the dining room. Give the severed limbs to an invisible chef, who gives you a silver key. Frog chain: nod to the frog and it joins you. It crosses a narrow bridge you can't use to flip a switch that unlocks the Mirror Room. You use it in the Mirror Room, and then you must push it through a door window to feed it to a giant snake so you can reach the next room. *Why it works:* The player does the cruel things with their own hands: cutting up a toy that bleeds, sacrificing a friendly companion. That makes them complicit and unsettled. A reviewer called the bear step cruel. My own reading, not stated in the sources: these acts take on new meaning once the twist reveals who the player really was.
- **Short scripted chases with simple escape rules**: Giant teddy bear: in the original, stepping back into the corridor ends it. In the MV you have to reach the presents room, and running into the basket room is a dead end. A small red skull chases you after you shelve the Book of Death; circle the bookshelf and leave the room. 5th floor: a skeleton in a cell, a giant skull, and Red Eyes. In 1.07 and the MV, being caught by the skeleton wakes you in the yellow-flower bedroom instead of killing you. Finale: the legless girl crawls after you surprisingly fast, and you must run at full speed. *Why it works:* The game is mostly slow, careful puzzle-solving, and short chases break that up with spikes of panic. Every chase has a readable rule, like a loop or a safe room, so escaping feels like you solved it rather than got lucky. A 2013 blog singled out the chase music as excellent and heart-pounding.
- **Timed hazards**: Dining room: a sound from the stairs starts a 5-second timer, and you must climb the fireplace before it runs out. 5th-floor poison hallway: you need colorless shoes. Get the Red Shoes from a cell after the white-pollen puzzle and wash them in the water chamber to turn them into Glass Shoes. Then you have 15 seconds to cross. If you are still standing on poison when the timer hits zero, Viola evaporates. A Viola corpse falls from the ceiling mid-run, and stepping on it slows you down. *Why it works:* A timer forces you to plan the route before you move and then carry it out under pressure. The falling corpse is a shock that also costs you time.
- **Scares that do nothing, and monsters that move when you look away**: Indie Hell Zone praised the small scares that exist only to unnerve and never hurt you, such as changes to rooms and glimpses of the witch. TV Tropes notes that monsters tend to move while the player isn't looking. One TV Tropes review says the lighting and sound build dread with simple but well-considered tricks. Example: after you give the statue its ring and leave, you hear a loud crash, and when you come back the statue has collapsed. *Why it works:* Mixing harmless scares with real instant deaths means the player can never tell which kind is coming. So they treat everything as dangerous, which keeps the dread going between actual deaths.
- **Diary storytelling**: Pages from Ellen's diary, the Witch's Diary, are scattered through the house. They tell Ellen's history step by step: lonely, too sick for anyone to play with her, ignored by her parents. She killed them, and a demon ate their souls, led her to the forest and gave her the house. She made a deal with the demon, which promised a cure if she let it eat more people. She got a body-switching spell, befriended Viola, and planned to borrow Viola's body 'just for a day'. The last diary, in the witch's room, talks about her 'friendship' with Viola. In the secret ending this entry is longer and describes the body swap in detail. A walkthrough tip: save after reading each journal, because dying afterwards can cost you the story. *Why it works:* The story arrives in small, optional pieces that reward exploring and give each floor a reason to exist. Because the diaries are Ellen's, the player reads the villain's mind the whole game without realizing who they are actually playing.
- **Endings that depend on a hidden action during the final chase**: Normal ending: run out the front door. Outside, Viola's father shoots the 'witch' chasing her. True ending: during the chase, skip the front door and go back to the 1st-floor room where you got the teddy bear. A cabinet there, which earlier only said to come back when the house is normal, now holds Ellen's Knife. Grab it while staying ahead of the chaser, then leave; the knife is used on the witch's eye and the reveal plays. Secret ending: never save or talk to the cat. The witch's room is then labelled 'My Room' and its item descriptions read as if their owner is examining them. MV hidden ending: wait on the first screen for an hour. The cat gets bored at 10 minutes and leaves at 50. The fan wiki says an extra ending was added in v1.07. *Why it works:* The true ending sits behind an action that goes against survival instinct: turning back into danger instead of escaping. Curious players get rewarded. The normal ending looks like a win on first play, which sets players up for the shock when the true ending reveals what really happened.

### Puzzle examples

- (The Witch's House) Opening: roses block the forest exit. A hidden path left of the big rose area holds a shiny item, a machete. It must be used on the SMALL rose patch near the start; using it on the large patch breaks the blade into Viola's skull. In 1.06 the machete breaks after one cut. A 'Cute Little Bottle' found later is the real answer to the large roses, used after you escape the house. *Lesson:* Teach 'items have one correct target and the wrong one kills you' in the first five minutes. Keep the obvious, larger obstacle as a fake-out and pay it off later with a different item.
- (The Witch's House) First room of the house: a blood stain on the floor. Walking over it makes the walls close in and crush you. Walking around it is safe. *Lesson:* The first trap is the clearest warning sign in the game. One death teaches the player to read the floor and walls for the rest of it.
- (The Witch's House) Teddy bear chain across three rooms: bear from the presents room → chained scissors on a workbench cut off its limbs → torso goes in the basket under the 'BEARS IN THE BASKET' note → dining room opens. Severed limbs go to an invisible chef → silver key. Coming back to the cat's room sets off a giant-bear chase; escape to the presents room, not the basket room. *Lesson:* One item splits into two pieces that solve two puzzles. The written note gives the rule ('bear must fit'), and the player works out the grim method. A chase on the way back keeps a solved area from feeling safe.
- (The Witch's House) Library loop on the 2nd floor: the librarian wants something to bind books. Moving barrels in the spider-room hallway reveals a note; searching the barrels finds rope. Rope → librarian gives the Book of Death. Choose Cancel when asked to read it (reading = death). Shelve it on the lone bookshelf in the glass display room → a small red skull chases you; loop around the shelf and leave. *Lesson:* A fetch quest ends in a dangerous object with a clear yes/no temptation. The danger is in the player's choice, not in reflexes. A short chase follows right after solving it, so the reward is also a scare.
- (The Witch's House) Spider and butterfly: a real yellow butterfly is stuck in a spider's web. Swap in the blue butterfly model from the glass case. The spider is colorblind, so it doesn't notice. *Lesson:* A fact about a creature, learned from the world, makes a decoy swap feel clever. Swapping a fake for the real thing is a pattern worth reusing.
- (The Witch's House) Frog companion, 3rd floor: nod to recruit the frog. It crosses a narrow bridge in the hallway to flip a lever that unlocks the Mirror Room. In the Mirror Room, make the right half match the left: frog on the middle-left chair; cake from the central cabinet on the right table, turned to point like a right arrow; fork from the left shelves on the right plate; move the plant; rearrange the shelves; swap the painting; rose in the left vase. Tear the wall note off LAST, then sit in the chair by the right table to unlock the door. Afterwards, look through a door window and push the frog through to feed the snake. In Extra mode the frog escapes, so you need a stuffed frog. The Baidu wiki says opening the snake door without a frog gets you eaten. *Lesson:* A companion who is useful early is sacrificed later, which hits harder because you used them. A symmetry room makes the player compare the whole screen carefully, and the 'tear the note last' order rule rewards care.
- (The Witch's House) 4th floor: a woman statue says her ring is missing. The ring is in a pot in the kitchen. Give it back to continue; you're warned not to enter the room next to her early. When you leave, a crash sounds, and when you return the statue has collapsed. A grandfather clock asks a riddle whose answer is 'clock' and gives you the Queen Key. *Lesson:* The fetch item comes from an unrelated room, which links areas together. Change a room after the player solves it, with a sound cue first, so finished areas still feel unsafe.
- (The Witch's House) Eye wall: three faces are drawn on a wall, and a note says to go where only one eye is open. Stand between a closed-eyed face and an open-eyed face so only one eye is in view, then search the wall to reveal a door. In MV Extra, all the eyes are open, the mouth spot is a death trap, and the answer is on the opposite wall, which the top-down view hides. *Lesson:* A written rule plus a reading of the room's art that the player has to work out. A remix can use the camera's blind spots to surprise people who think they know the answer.
- (The Witch's House) 5th floor: the poison hallway needs colorless shoes. Get the Red Shoes from a cell after the white-pollen puzzle and wash them in the water chamber to make Glass Shoes. You get 15 seconds to cross, and a corpse falls mid-run and slows you if you step on it. Elsewhere, talking to the red grasses gives the answer to an 'X the white flower' prompt. Fill an empty bottle at the purple poison river and use it on the White Flower. Talking to the yellow flowers gets you White Powder. *Lesson:* Change an item's state (red → washed → glass) to open a hazard. Talking plant NPCs give the answer as dialogue. A timed crossing is the floor's final exam, and the extra mid-run scare is also a mechanical obstacle.
- (The Witch's House) Final chase and true ending: after the witch's room, a legless girl chases you down through the house. Earlier, a cabinet in the 1st-floor teddy-bear room said to come back when the house is normal. Now it opens and holds Ellen's Knife. Detour there under pursuit, then leave by the front door. *Lesson:* Leave an unexplained 'not yet' lock early and only open it during the climax. The best ending goes to players who remember it and take a risk instead of running.

### Atmosphere and pacing

- Abrupt opening: no introduction. Viola wakes in the forest and the roses already block the exit. One reviewer found this jarring at first but not off-putting.
- Floor-by-floor structure (1st to 5th, plus forest and garden). Each floor is a small cluster of linked puzzles; reviewers describe the house as 'ever-changing'.
- Fear comes from anticipation and foreshadowing, not from a monster stalking you (The Boar). Because 'anything can kill you', even ordinary interactions are tense.
- Mixed rhythm: careful exploration and puzzles, broken up by sudden instant deaths, short scripted chases, and 5- and 15-second timers.
- Harmless scares (room changes, glimpses of the witch) are mixed with real threats. Monsters move when you aren't looking. A sound comes first and the change shows up when you return, as with the crash and the collapsed statue.
- Lighting and sound use simple but well-considered tricks. Chases are announced by a sound or a changed object, and the chase music raises your heart rate.
- Escalation toward the finale: the save cat is found dead just before the witch's room. Then the last diary is read, and the game ends on a chase back down through every floor you have explored.
- 1.07 and the MV soften some punishments: being caught by the skeleton wakes you in the yellow-flower bedroom instead of killing you. That gives the player a breather in the hardest stretch.

### Story structure

- Premise: Viola, 13, visits her friend Ellen, who lives in woods rumoured to be haunted by a witch who kidnaps children. Viola wakes in the forest, roses block the way home, and she enters the house.
- Middle: Viola climbs the house floor by floor. Witch's Diary pages fill in Ellen's past in order: illness, loneliness, neglectful parents, killing them, a demon eating their souls and giving her the house, the pact for a cure in exchange for more victims, the body-switch spell, choosing Viola as her 'friend'.
- The cat is a companion throughout. Its corpse appears right before the witch's room, a signal that the story is about to turn.
- Climax: the witch's room and the final diary about the girls' 'friendship', then a chase by a legless, eyeless girl. Ellen had cut off her own legs and gouged out her eyes before the swap so that Viola, trapped in Ellen's body, couldn't follow or escape.
- Normal ending: Viola gets out, and her father shoots the 'witch' crawling after her. It plays like a win the first time.
- True ending (Ellen's Knife): the reveal that the character you played was Ellen inside Viola's body all along. The thing chasing you was the real Viola. Her father doesn't recognize her, calls her a monster, shoots her, and walks off with a giggling 'Viola'. One source says Viola loses hope and with it the immortality of the witch's body. Another source says the real Viola uses the magic left in Ellen's body to block the exit with roses. Sources disagree on details.
- Secret ending: the witch's room is labelled 'My Room', item descriptions are written as if their owner is looking at them, the diary is longer and explicit about the swap, and the cat is shown to be the demon.
- Why it shocks (my analysis from the sources): the twist recasts the hunter as the victim and the player as the villain. It also makes the 'happy' normal ending a murder by a father of his own daughter. Pentucket News calls it the biggest plot twist among the classic RPG Maker horror games.
- Expanded story: Fummy wrote a prequel novel, The Diary of Ellen (2 November 2013), adapted into a nine-part manga drawn by Yuna Kagesaki starting in 2017.

### Art and audio

- Made in RPG Maker VX: top-down tile maps with small chibi character sprites. One reviewer found the VX graphics much cleaner and easier to read than older RPG Maker horror like Ib.
- Reviewers call the pixel art basic, but say its limits support the storytelling and scares instead of weakening them.
- Gore shows up through small details and event scenes: blood smears that double as warnings, a teddy bear whose cut limbs bleed, a pantry of skeletons, and a unique death scene for each trap.
- Visual warnings are built into the tiles: blood stains on the floor and memos on walls are both art and gameplay clues.
- Sound is used for warnings: chases and room changes are announced by a sound (a crash, noise from the stairs). One 2013 blog says the background music fades away but the chase music is excellent.
- One TV Tropes reviewer says simple, well-considered lighting and sound tricks build dread, though there are a few too many jump scares.
- MV remake: Fummy redrew all the graphics alone, which took about five times as long as the original. Fummy turned down co-production offers to keep the atmosphere. A ResetEra user said the songs were redone to sound like the originals but better, and praised the new sprites.

### Criticisms and pitfalls

- Some puzzles were criticized as vague and complicated (Jay Is Games, as summarized by Wikipedia).
- Some deaths come 'out of nowhere' and you can't know about them in advance. They hurt if you haven't saved at a cat recently (WayTooManyGames). Lesson: keep saves close together, or add an Easy-style retry.
- Too many jump scares, and they become expected after the first few (TV Tropes review, The Boar). Lesson: vary the scares and keep some that do nothing.
- RPGSite (6/10, MV) felt no real sense of danger or urgency while exploring, and called it 'okay but nothing spectacular'.
- Short and linear: a Medium reviewer said the length didn't justify the $15 price of the MV.
- Story you can miss: dying after reading a diary before saving can cost you the lore.
- Secret-ending conditions (never saving vs never talking to the cat) are unclear and differ between versions. One MV player saved once and still missed it. Lesson: hidden-ending rules should be clear once you find out about them, and consistent.
- The abrupt opening with no introduction can jar new players.
- Puzzle solutions and endings changed a lot between versions (1.06, 1.07, MV), so walkthroughs conflict. This is a warning to keep one clear design document.

### Playtime and scope

The original is short. Indie Hell Zone describes about a one-hour run, Wikipedia (via Jay Is Games) calls it a short evening adventure, and reviewers call it short and linear. Scope: one solo developer and freeware, built in RPG Maker VX. The map is a forest and garden plus a five-floor house, with roughly 3–5 linked puzzles per floor. The cast is very small: Viola/Ellen, the black cat, a few creature NPCs (frog, snake, spider, invisible chef, librarian, woman statue, talking red grasses and yellow flowers, teddy bear), 3–4 chasers, and the father in the endings. Endings: Normal, True and Secret, plus one added in v1.07 according to an old wiki revision. The MV also has a hidden ending where you wait on the first screen for an hour. Replay value comes from the knife and no-save endings and, in the MV, from the remixed Extra mode. To reach a 1–2 hour target, a similar game could use about 5–6 themed areas, roughly 4 puzzle chains each, a save point every few rooms, and an optional remix mode or extra endings for replay.

## The broader RPG Maker / Wolf RPG Editor freeware horror genre (Misao, Corpse Party PC-98, The Crooked Man, Paranoiac, Yume Nikki, OneShot, Mogeko Castle, Seven Mysteries, plus Ib and The Witch's House for comparison): shared conventions, concrete puzzle and mechanic structures, and why the games spread

Classic Japanese freeware horror is "explorer horror": a top-down tile-based adventure with no real combat. You flee threats instead of fighting them, and nearly all progress comes from finding a key item and using it in the right spot. Notes and diaries supply both lore and puzzle codes, and lock or code puzzles are clued one or two rooms away. Chases and hiding spots provide the action, instant deaths and "wrong/bad ends" provide the stakes, and several endings decided by choices or hidden conditions keep players coming back. The games spread because they were free and easy to translate (Yume Nikki on 2channel in 2004, then fan translations; vgperson's English versions of Misao, Mad Father, The Crooked Man and others). Ao Oni's Niconico play-through culture took it past 50 million views by 2013, and Western YouTubers (PewDiePie in 2011, Markiplier) carried it abroad. ### Mechanics

- **Key-item inventory used on a hotspot (the core verb)**: The inventory is a short list of key items, and progress means picking the right item and using it on the right tile. A Misao reviewer: most puzzles 'don't go beyond picking out an item from the Key Items inventory and using it in a certain spot.' Mogeko Castle's remake shows each item as a picture in a bubble. Keys often unlock many locks: in Mogeko Castle one key from the 2F fairy frees Defect Mogeko's shackles, and most keys in the castle are shared. *Why it works:* It keeps controls to walking and pressing one button, which suits RPG Maker and keeps the player's attention on the room rather than on menus. The tension comes from knowing you have an item and searching for where it belongs while something might be hunting you.
- **Multi-step item chains across rooms**: One item opens a container that holds a clue, and that clue opens a different door. Crooked Man, Central Hospital stage: take a wire from the cans in the cafeteria → use it on a locker behind the desk → the note inside gives code 4489 → the code opens the outpatient clinic. In the same stage, a lockbox at the nurse's station (press the middle square, then the four corners) gives both the cafeteria key and the elevator trunk key. *Why it works:* The chain makes the building feel connected and turns the floor plan into the puzzle. Each link is simple, but holding three or four of them in mind across rooms feels like real deduction.
- **Codes and passwords clued by documents**: Numeric or word locks whose answer is hidden in a note, journal or book elsewhere. Crooked Man: a passcode door on 1F west is opened with 3679, clued by a note mentioning missing chapters (with a Jekyll and Hyde hint). John Keith's combination security box (1647) holds the lecture-hallway key, and most professors own such a box, so the motif repeats. A puzzle box requires spelling a word; its note says it is 'something you could not live without', and the answer is FAMILY, which ties the solution to the game's theme. In Misao, a password for the true ending is learned by talking to Mr. Onigawara and choosing an invisible third dialogue option. *Why it works:* Reading notes is both the lore delivery and the puzzle, so players read everything. A thematic answer such as FAMILY makes solving the puzzle restate the story.
- **Hiding spots during a timed hunt**: Paranoiac: during the night chases, candidate hiding places are marked with pink arrows. Only one is correct each night, and you can enter a spot only in the few seconds before the monster comes into the room. Of the wrong spots, two kill you outright and the rest get you found, so you must keep running and lose health. Hiding spots include under a kotatsu and a cabinet beside a mirrored dresser. Taking pills in the kitchen during the day raises the next chase's health from 3 to 5. *Why it works:* It is hide-and-seek with death, the same core as Ao Oni and LUNA. Daytime preparation (pills) feeds night survival, so the calm half of the loop has a mechanical payoff.
- **Day/night loop in one house**: Paranoiac covers five days and nights in a single house. By day the player explores and solves puzzles to collect keys; the dead aunt locked every door and hid the keys because of her paranoia, which explains in-story why everything is locked. By night a creature hunts you and you must hide. *Why it works:* A small map is reused for alternating moods, which makes for cheap content and growing dread. The locked-everything puzzle design gets a story reason instead of being arbitrary.
- **Scripted chase sequences and the door maze**: Mogeko Castle: corridors where enemies spawn and chase you; picking the wrong door or direction ends the run, with no signal of which is right. One door maze has a fixed answer sequence (middle, left, right, middle, middle). Crooked Man has one-on-one confrontations: lead him in a straight line, swing the knife until it lands, then run; one fight needs 20 hits. *Why it works:* Chases break up slow exploration with sudden action, and fixed scripted chases are easy to build in RPG Maker. The flip side is that pure memorisation feels unfair (see criticisms).
- **Health as a story object**: Ib: health is a rose. Ib's red rose has 5 petals (it starts with 3), Garry's blue rose has 10, and Mary's rose is fake. Petals fall to enemies, hazards, some dialogue and failed puzzles, and fixed reusable vases refill them. Paranoiac uses a small hit count (3, or 5 with pills). *Why it works:* The health bar is part of the world and the theme, so damage feels personal. Fixed refill points keep the health economy manageable while the threat stays constant.
- **Instant death, wrong ends and a death collection**: The Witch's House: traps at almost every turn, such as sliding walls or a flower that impales; deaths often come without warning. Misao has 40+ death or bad endings, and a list on 1F next to Mr. Onigawara counts how many deaths you have seen (one achievement wants 40 of 42). In Corpse Party, 'wrong ends' come from small choices or missed steps, such as not checking a mirror, and some wrong ends reveal extra lore about the school. *Why it works:* Death becomes content: each one is a short horror vignette or a piece of lore, so failing still rewards the player. The collection list encourages players to go looking for deaths.
- **Save points in the world and save rules**: Yume Nikki: you save by sitting at the desk and writing in the dream diary, and you enter the dream world by sleeping in the bed. The Witch's House: a talking black cat is the save point (Normal mode); late in the game the cat's corpse becomes the save point, and the secret ending needs a run with no saves at all, which reveals who the cat really is. The MV remake's Easy mode respawns you at the start of the room where you died. Crooked Man lets you save almost anywhere, which reviewers call helpful given how often you die. A Misao reviewer says its quicksave makes the game less tense. *Why it works:* An in-world save object makes saving part of the fiction and can later become a story twist. How often you can save sets how tense the game feels: rare saves raise dread, and save-anywhere lowers frustration but also tension.
- **Multiple endings decided by dialogue and hidden conditions**: Crooked Man: specific lines at set points cause bad ends ('Don't cry' → Bad End 1 at Hotel Ruhenheim; 'You shouldn't give up' → Bad End 2; on the rooftop 'Persuade' → Bad End 3, 'Take the gun' → Bad End 4). Misao: the final choice of which of two people to sacrifice decides the bad or good ending. Paranoiac: the good ending needs Miki to believe the 'hallucination' is real. Corpse Party PC-98: the best ending if all five survive, a darker one if anyone dies. Seven Mysteries: True, Happy and three Bad endings. Mogeko Castle: about eight bad endings plus Normal and True. *Why it works:* Story choices carry weight, and replaying for the true ending can double the play time cheaply. When the deciding choice reflects the story's theme (believing versus dismissing, mercy versus revenge), the ending reads as a verdict on the player.
- **Collect-N macro goal with a hub**: Misao: find 6 body parts (brain, head, eyes, arms, legs, heart) in roughly any order and bring them to an altar outside. Yume Nikki: the Nexus is a hub of doors to themed dream worlds; collect 24 'effects' and drop them in the Nexus to unlock the ending, where a stepladder appears on the real-world balcony. *Why it works:* One clear long-term goal plus non-linear order gives the player freedom without leaving them lost. Each part or effect makes a self-contained sub-area with its own puzzle chain.
- **Abilities that change how you move**: Yume Nikki's effects change Madotsuki's sprite and abilities: the Bicycle doubles walking speed (to catch fast characters and escape monsters), and the Frog lets her walk at normal speed through water. Most effects are optional, but some are needed for missable scenes. *Why it works:* Collecting ability items opens up the map like a small Metroidvania, and the sprite change is visible feedback that costs little art.
- **Hint character**: Misao: once you befriend 'Ms. Library' in the 2F Library, you can ask her for hints whenever you like, which is the game's built-in hint system. *Why it works:* Lets a puzzle-heavy game stay hard without driving players to walkthroughs, and keeps the hints in-world.
- **Meta and fourth-wall elements**: OneShot (PC): many puzzles need the player to act outside the game, in the real OS. A character says the hint is in 'the easiest accessible space on your computer' (the desktop); the wallpaper changes to reveal a clue; a file in the game folder gives a safe password; a remote-control code is randomised every run (four coloured numbers seen in a window, entered by colour order). Quitting at a key moment (2014 freeware version) triggers a separate ending, after which the title screen shows the lightbulb smashed with only Niko's hat and scarf left. On a New Game+ run Niko knows the player's name without knowing why. The console/World Machine edition uses a fake in-game OS instead. The Witch's House hides its secret ending behind never saving. *Why it works:* The player becomes a character in the story, and a game that 'remembers' or reaches outside its window breaks the safety of the screen. In a browser game the same effect can come from things the game itself controls: the tab title, local save data, the system clock, or a fake desktop.
- **Rare random events**: Yume Nikki: a ghost face has a 1-in-64 chance of appearing each time a certain light switch is flipped. OneShot's remote code is randomised per run. *Why it works:* Rare scares seen by only some players start rumours and keep the world unpredictable on replays.
- **Switching characters or perspectives**: Ib has puzzles where you switch between characters. Seven Mysteries changes protagonist by chapter (Sang in Chapter 1, Tuan from Chapter 4). Corpse Party follows a party of five split up. *Why it works:* The player sees the same mystery from more than one side, which supports twists, and the protagonist can be unreliable.

### Puzzle examples

- (The Crooked Man) Central Hospital chain: wire from the cafeteria cans → opens a locker behind the desk → the note inside has code 4489 → opens the outpatient clinic. A nurse's-station lockbox (press middle, then the four corners) gives two keys at once. *Lesson:* Build three- or four-link chains across neighbouring rooms, and let one solve pay out more than one item so the map opens up in a burst.
- (The Crooked Man) A puzzle box needs a spelled word; its note says it is 'something you could not live without', and the answer, FAMILY, sums up the game's emotional theme. The box/safe holds a revolver and ammo. *Lesson:* Make the answer to a word lock the theme of the story, so solving it is a small reveal. Reward it with an item that changes the next confrontation.
- (The Crooked Man) A passcode door (3679) whose clue is a note about 'missing chapters' (with a Jekyll and Hyde allusion), and a professor's combination security box (1647). Most professors own such a box, so the lock type repeats with a different clue each time. *Lesson:* Repeat one lock type with fresh clue sources so players learn the grammar and then apply it, and use literary or diegetic texts as the clue.
- (The Crooked Man) Dialogue choices act as hidden pass/fail gates: replying 'Don't cry' or 'You shouldn't give up' at key moments causes immediate bad ends; the rooftop choice between 'Persuade' and 'Take the gun' decides Bad Ends 3 and 4. *Lesson:* Choices in conversation can be puzzles of empathy: the 'correct' line follows from understanding the character's grief, not from guessing.
- (Paranoiac) Each night several pink-arrow hiding spots appear; only one is safe, and you must commit in the seconds before the monster enters. Daytime pills raise your night health from 3 to 5. *Lesson:* Tie daytime preparation to night survival. But give tells (sound, light, a story clue) for the correct spot, because players criticised pure guessing.
- (Paranoiac) Daytime key hunts in a house where the paranoid aunt locked every door and hid the keys, e.g. 'examine the right side of the cabinet in the far-left corner' to find a key. *Lesson:* Give the lock-and-key structure an in-story reason drawn from a character's mental state; the puzzle structure then characterises someone who is absent.
- (Misao) Six body parts, collected in roughly any order, each through its own sub-chain (e.g. free a teacher from a 2F classroom locker for the brain; take a bat from a 1F hall locker for the head and legs; check desks marked A, B and C to find vases for the eyes; salt from a mandrake is a component of the heart). *Lesson:* A non-linear 'collect N' hub lets stuck players switch to another thread instead of hitting a wall; ideal for a 1–2 hour game.
- (Misao) The true-ending password is learned by choosing an invisible third dialogue option with one NPC. *Lesson:* Hide true-ending secrets inside the game's own interface, rewarding players who suspect the menus themselves.
- (Mogeko Castle) A door maze with a fixed sequence (middle, left, right, middle, middle) while being chased; a wrong door is a game over. *Lesson:* A memorised sequence alone feels like trial and error. If you use one, clue it somewhere (a rhyme, a painting, a note) so the chase tests nerve and memory, not luck.
- (Ib) Paintings hide codes; a logic puzzle asks which of several statue or painting statements is the liar; blue paint leaking from a frame later forms a message pointing to another painting; some puzzles need switching characters. *Lesson:* Make the setting (a gallery) the source of puzzle content, and mix logic puzzles with search puzzles for variety.
- (OneShot) The game tells the player the hint is on 'the easiest accessible space on your computer'. The desktop wallpaper changes to show a clue; a file found outside the game window gives the safe password; a randomised four-digit coloured code seen through a window is entered at a computer by colour order. *Lesson:* One or two 'outside the box' puzzles near the climax can define a game. A browser game can copy the idea with in-game copies of things like a fake OS, the tab title or saved notes, without real system access.
- (Yume Nikki) No traditional puzzles: you explore a hub of doors to find 24 effects (Bicycle = double speed, Frog = walk through water); depositing all of them in the Nexus unlocks the balcony ending. *Lesson:* Surreal exploration areas can be the calmer, unsettling counterweight to tight puzzle rooms; ability items can unlock places you already passed.
- (Corpse Party (PC-98 / Blood Covered)) Wrong ends caused by small missed actions (e.g. not checking a mirror) lock the player into a bad outcome; some wrong ends show extra lore. *Lesson:* Make bad ends teach or reveal something, but warn or checkpoint the player so a missed step does not cost 30–40 minutes.
- (The Witch's House) Many rooms are trap puzzles where touching the wrong object kills you instantly; saving is done at a black cat, and a secret ending requires never saving. *Lesson:* Instant-death rooms work if death is fast and a save or respawn is close; the save mechanic itself can be a twist.

### Atmosphere and pacing

- Explorer-horror rule: enemies usually cannot be beaten, only escaped; the closest thing to a boss fight is a chase where you run and hide (TV Tropes 'Explorer Horror'). The tool's menu-only combat pushed designers toward collecting objects and away from fighting.
- Atmosphere is built from art direction and sound design rather than visual detail: a setting that 'feels off' and strange monsters work despite simple tiles (indiehellzone). Ao Oni's background music is singled out as a key part of its atmosphere despite how simple the game is.
- Calm stretches separated by sudden threats: Paranoiac alternates quiet day exploration with night hunts; Crooked Man reviewers say dying or a sudden bad end is easy at any moment; Witch's House deaths arrive before you understand what happened.
- Powerlessness as theme: Yume Nikki's lack of goals and of tools that do much makes the player feel 'as powerless and isolated' as Madotsuki; it is often read as being about social isolation and depression.
- How often you can save sets the tension: Misao's quicksave lets players mash save before every room, which a reviewer says lowers tension; Witch's House's sparse cat saves make every room nerve-wracking.
- Tone management matters: Mogeko Castle starts as a 'hilarious horror comedy' but reviewers felt it lost its charm as it shifted darker; mixing humour and horror needs a deliberate turning point.
- The horror is often human: retrospectives say that although supernatural elements appear, 'the real horror came from human actions'. The Witch's House is famous for its final twist.
- Optional lore slows the pace for those who want it: The Witch's House's diary pages reveal the prequel story slowly for curious players, while players focused on survival can skip them.
- Rare random scares (Yume Nikki's 1-in-64 ghost face on a light switch) keep familiar rooms from feeling safe on revisits.

### Story structure

- Location-based acts: The Crooked Man is split into numbered scenes or stages, each set in a distinct building (Hotel Ruhenheim, Julius Stone Law School, Central Hospital, then a rooftop climax), each with its own key-and-code puzzle set and its own possible bad ends.
- Time-boxed acts in one place: Paranoiac covers 5 days and nights in a single inherited house; each day adds story and each night tests survival.
- Chapters with different protagonists: Seven Mysteries (cursed school; Sang in Ch.1, Tuan from Ch.4; the remake 'The Last Page' has 11 chapters) and Corpse Party (a party of five; Blood Covered released in five chapters 2007–2011) use point-of-view shifts to set up twists.
- A goal with a moral sting: Misao has the player gather a murdered girl's six body parts for an altar, then choose which of two people who wronged her to sacrifice; that choice decides the bad or good ending.
- Ambiguity as story: Yume Nikki has no dialogue-driven plot; meaning comes from imagery and a single ending, which fans have debated for over a decade.
- Unreliable mind: Paranoiac's protagonist has a history of mental illness and an abusive mother, and the good ending needs her to believe the 'hallucination' is real, so the ending choice is about trusting her own perception.
- Endings ranked by outcome: Corpse Party PC-98's best ending requires all five to survive and is noted as the first to rank its endings with school-style grades; otherwise survivors stay haunted. Seven Mysteries has True, Happy and three Bad endings; Mogeko Castle about eight bad endings plus Normal and True.
- The player as character: OneShot treats the player as separate from the protagonist Niko; Niko speaks to the player directly, and the game remembers previous runs (the player's name on NG+, a smashed-lightbulb title screen after quitting).
- Prequel or lore told through found documents: The Witch's House's diary pages and Crooked Man's professors' journals carry backstory alongside puzzle clues.

### Art and audio

- Engines: RPG Maker 2003 (Yume Nikki), RPG Maker VX Ace (Seven Mysteries), WOLF RPG Editor (Misao, The Crooked Man, Paranoiac; Japanese-only and more complex than RPG Maker), and the original PC-98 'RPG Tsukūru Dante 98' (Corpse Party 1996). The look: 3/4 top-down tiles, small sprites, simple animations.
- Tile-based maps with room-by-room event scripting suit horror built on exploration. Reviewers praised The Crooked Man's 'large, detailed levels' and cohesive atmosphere despite 'standard RPG-style pixel art'.
- Sprite variation as feedback: Yume Nikki's effects change the protagonist's costume or sprite, which shows the player's state at a glance.
- Item presentation: the Mogeko Castle remake shows inventory items as pictures inside bubbles. Tied Up (a modern RPG Maker horror game) was praised for marking pickups as big bright stars so they can't be missed, which counters pixel hunting.
- Audio: Ao Oni's background music is credited as a major contributor to its effect; the Corpse Party Blood Covered remake added amateur voice acting. Yume Nikki: Dream Diary was praised for visuals, music and atmosphere even though critics disliked its gameplay. (Detailed sound-design research was cut short when the search budget ran out.)
- Old-engine limits (limited colour depth and hard-coded menu text in early RPG Maker, per vgperson) forced simple styles that read clearly; designers focused on pacing, atmosphere and the unknown instead of graphical fidelity.
- Jump scares come through sudden sprite or event changes and instant-death animations (The Witch's House's jump scares are a highlight for reviewers), though long death cutscenes annoy players on repeated deaths.

### Criticisms and pitfalls

- Random hiding spots: TV Tropes on Paranoiac says there's no way to predict the correct spot, so survival depends on chance more than skill or deduction, and a wrong spot can mean instant death. Fix: give readable tells.
- Unclued wrong doors in chases: Mogeko Castle chases have no indication of the correct door, and a Backloggd reviewer calls them '100% scripted' with 'nearly zero player agency'. Reviewers advise saving after every successful chase.
- Obscure bad-end triggers: Corpse Party reviewers say obscure wrong-end triggers are 'very frustrating' and can send you back 30–40 minutes; one wrong end was forced simply because the player hadn't checked a mirror.
- No map and aimless wandering: Corpse Party Blood Covered criticised for the lack of a proper map; Misao's sub-objectives are 'too discreet', causing aimless wandering. Fix: an objective line, a hint character, or a map.
- Puzzles that rely on memorisation or trial and error (Misao reviewers) and backtracking to hunt for 'the switch to flip' (itch.io players) wear out patience.
- Save design extremes: unlimited quicksave kills tension (Misao); scarce saves with surprise instant death cause frustration (Witch's House players lose progress without a recent cat save). An Easy-mode respawn at the start of the room is a good middle ground.
- Long, unskippable death scenes become annoying when deaths repeat.
- Tonal drift and excess: Mogeko Castle's comedy-to-dark shift lost reviewers, and its gore and sexual content drew warnings; Misao's deaths struck some as over the top and almost parody. Implying rather than showing ('leaving the gory details to the observer's mind') was praised.
- Over-large maps and weak localisation: Seven Mysteries players asked for tighter maps to keep exploration from becoming a chore, and for proofread English.
- Story over gameplay: PopMatters felt The Crooked Man's gameplay was secondary to its story, and Rice Digital found Mogeko Castle's puzzles 'sparse and quite easy'. For a puzzle-focused game, puzzle density must be designed on purpose.
- Real-OS meta tricks (OneShot desktop puzzles) don't carry over to other platforms; OneShot's console version needed a fake in-game OS. A browser game should simulate meta effects inside the game.

### Playtime and scope

The classics are short, tightly packed games. GameFAQs users report about 4 hours for The Witch's House (2012, 49 users) and about 6 hours for The Witch's House MV (12 users), while Steam commenters cite 2–3 hours for a quick run. A Spanish blog says Paranoiac 'is not that long'; it is structured as 5 day/night cycles in one house. The Crooked Man spans several building-sized stages. Corpse Party Blood Covered was split into 5 chapters, and its Chapter 5 is called overly long. The Seven Mysteries remake has 11 chapters. Much of the length comes from deaths, retries and replays for other endings rather than unique content (Misao: 40+ deaths; Mogeko Castle: about 8 bad ends plus Normal and True). HowLongToBeat pages could not be reached, so there are no verified main-story figures for Misao, Crooked Man, Mogeko Castle or Paranoiac. Scope guidance for a 1–2 hour original, inferred from these games: 3–5 location acts or chapters, each with one major multi-step puzzle chain plus 2–4 smaller locks or codes; one recurring stalker with 3–6 chases or hiding events, each with readable tells; nearby saves or room-start respawn; 1 non-linear 'collect N' section; and 2–3 endings (bad, normal, true) plus several quick, optional death vignettes.

## Puzzle design craft for RPG Maker / adventure-style psychological horror: lock-and-key chains, environmental code puzzles, push blocks, piano/sound, painting, mirror, timed escape and item puzzles; avoiding moon logic, signposting, puzzle density, hub-and-wing gating, backtracking

Every WebFetch failed with a DNS error (Wikipedia, Fandom, Game Developer, Grumpy Gamer, Steam, Reddit), and a direct HTTPS request was refused by the session's egress proxy. Everything below therefore comes from my background knowledge, not from pages read this session. I mark details I'm unsure of as 'from memory/unverified', and the designer should check those before relying on them. The main craft points are well established: (1) structure the game as a puzzle dependency chart that branches, with a hub and locked wings; (2) always show the lock before the key, and give each solution two or three redundant clues; (3) mark what can be interacted with and reward the player's intent, so there are no pixel hunts or moon logic; (4) make backtracking tense by changing the world and moving the pursuer, not by making it tedious; (5) never let a puzzle that needs hard thinking overlap with a chase, and make instant deaths fair with warnings and quick retries. These are the habits that make Ao Oni, Ib, The Witch's House and Mad Father work, and their most common failures (trial-and-error deaths, obscure clues, dull key-fetching) are what to avoid.


### Mechanics

- **Hub with locked wings (emblem/crest gating)**: A central safe-ish hub (foyer/main hall) has 3–4 visibly locked doors, each needing a distinct, named token (e.g. 'a door with a moth-shaped keyhole'). Each wing has its own short internal chain of puzzles that ends in a token for another hub door, often plus a shortcut back to the hub. The best-known model is Resident Evil (1996)'s mansion, where doors carry crest symbols (armor/sword/helmet/shield keys, from memory) and emblems are placed in hub objects. RPG Maker horror mansions (Ao Oni, Mad Father) use the same pattern in smaller form: locked doors whose keys are found deeper in the house. *Why it works:* The player always knows the big goal ('open the remaining doors'), can see their progress (doors opened), and returns often to a place they know. Each return can be made unsettling. Wings can also be built and tested as separate modules, which matters for a 1–2 hour scope.
- **Lock before key (no backwards puzzles)**: Ron Gilbert's 1989 essay 'Why Adventure Games Suck' (from memory) warns against 'backwards puzzles', where the player finds the solution before meeting the problem, and asks that sub-goals be obvious. In practice the player sees the locked safe, the silent piano or the blocked hallway first. The diary with the code or the sheet music comes later, so finding it produces an immediate 'that's for the safe!' *Why it works:* The clue becomes a moment of recognition instead of a random item the player has to remember. In horror this also gives the player a reason to go back through dangerous rooms.
- **Puzzle dependency chart, kept branching**: Gilbert's 'Puzzle Dependency Charts' post (Grumpy Gamer, written during Thimbleweed Park; from memory) charts puzzles as nodes and 'must be solved before' as arrows, built backwards from the ending. A 'bushy' chart has several independent threads open at once that come together at gates. A long single chain is the thing to avoid. Rule of thumb: keep 2–3 unsolved puzzles available at any time, and narrow to 1 only for scripted set pieces. *Why it works:* When the player gets stuck on one thread they can make progress on another. This keeps frustration from replacing fear, and it lets the designer check that the game can be completed and has no soft-locks.
- **Environmental code puzzles with redundant clues**: A 3–4 digit code or a sequence is split across the world. Examples: a diary entry ('she was born the year the clock stopped'), a stopped clock showing 4:17, a child's drawing with three candles. The combination is a physical object on the map with a number dial UI. Following the 'Three Clue Rule' (Justin Alexander, The Alexandrian; from memory), give each conclusion two or three routes. For example, the year is in the diary and also carved on the grave in the garden. *Why it works:* Redundancy prevents a single missed note from blocking progress. Spreading the clues across rooms gives exploration a purpose, and having the clue come from a character's own writing ties the puzzle to the story instead of an arbitrary rule.
- **Auto-use items and minimal combining**: RPG Maker horror usually checks the inventory when the player interacts with something: walk up to the door while holding 'Rusty Key' and it is used automatically ('You used the Rusty Key.'). Picking from the menu or combining items is rare. Where combining exists, keep it to 1–3 obvious pairs (string + magnet, lighter + oil-soaked rag) and have the item description hint at it ('The magnet is too weak to reach down there by hand'). *Why it works:* Auto-use removes the 'try every item on everything' grind that leads to moon logic. The puzzle becomes about finding things and making deductions, which suits short horror games where frustration kills tension.
- **Pursuer chases tied to triggers, plus hiding spots**: In Ao Oni the monster appears in scripted moments: when you pick up a key item, open a particular door, or come back into a room. It then chases on a short timer or until you break line of sight. Closets and wardrobes let you hide (from memory: hiding is not always safe, which keeps you nervous). Trigger points are placed on backtracking routes and on the pickups that matter. *Why it works:* Because the threat is tied to progress, every key item feels dangerous to take. Players start to dread certain actions, so tension builds even in scenes with no chase.
- **Backtracking with changed state**: When the player returns to a solved area, something has changed: the lights are off, a portrait now faces the wall, a new handwritten note says 'YOU TOOK IT', furniture is moved, a corridor is flooded, or the pursuer patrols there. Opening a wing also unlocks a shortcut back to the hub, so the walk back is short. *Why it works:* It turns the necessary return trips of lock-and-key design from chores into scares, and it tells the player the house reacts to them.
- **Clear interactables and changing examine text**: Interactive objects get a sparkle, glint or distinct sprite. Everything else gives short flavour text when examined. The text changes once a related clue is known ('A clock stopped at 4:17.' becomes '4:17... the same as in the diary.'). The protagonist can also remark on objectives ('The basement door is chained. Maybe there's a bolt cutter in the shed.'). *Why it works:* It removes pixel hunting and confirms that the player's deduction is right, without simply handing over the answer.
- **Rewarding the player's intent and teaching failure**: Gilbert's 'reward intent' rule: if the player has the right idea, the game should accept it, or at least nudge them. Failures get specific messages ('The lock is too high to reach.' rather than 'Nothing happens.'). Graham Nelson's 'Bill of Player's Rights' (Craft of Adventure, 1995; from memory) adds: never get locked out of winning without warning, and never need knowledge from a past death to win. *Why it works:* The player feels clever, not lucky. Specific failure messages work as hints that fit inside the fiction.
- **Fair instant deaths**: The Witch's House is famous for frequent instant deaths: grabbed by a wall, crushed, eaten. It softens this with a talking black cat that acts as a save point, frequent saving, notes from the house that warn you, and deaths that are quick to retry. Mad Father (from memory) presents its deaths as collectible illustrated 'Dead End' scenes. *Why it works:* A death that was warned about feels like a lesson and becomes part of the fun. Collecting deaths turns failure into content. Without warnings and checkpoints, players see the same deaths as cheap trial and error.
- **Piano and sound puzzles**: A piano or music box needs a short sequence of 4–8 notes. The clue is a poem, sheet music or a diagram that maps to keys. Silent Hill (1999)'s school piano puzzle (from memory) uses a poem about birds, where silent birds stand for keys that make no sound. Resident Evil (1996) has a piano that plays a sheet-music piece to open a hidden room. In UI: show a labelled keyboard, play each note as it's pressed, and reset clearly when the sequence is wrong. *Why it works:* Sound gives feedback right away and adds atmosphere. A clue written as poetry fits the dread of the setting and can still be solved by reasoning.
- **Painting and picture puzzles**: Ib (2012) uses gallery paintings as both threats and signposts. Titles on wall plaques work as hints, paintings change or come out of their frames when you revisit, and the 'Lady' paintings chase you. Common patterns: a painting shows how a room should be arranged, a portrait's gaze or pointing hand marks a tile, a missing piece of a painting is an item to find, and the painting's title is the code word. *Why it works:* The environment carries the clue without extra text, and a thing the player trusted (a painting) becomes a threat in the same space.
- **Mirror puzzles**: Patterns: text written backwards that can only be read in a mirror; a mirror that shows the room in a different state (a door or object that exists only in the reflection, telling the player what to change); walking in a mirrored room where controls or layout are flipped; a reflection that lags or doesn't match the player as a scare before the puzzle. (These are general patterns, not taken from a single verified source.) *Why it works:* It creates two layers of reality at little cost, which suits a story about perception and guilt, and it can be built in RPG Maker style from duplicated tiles.
- **Push and slide block puzzles**: A staple of RPG Maker: push statues or crates onto pressure plates, or slide them on ice or a polished floor. Keep them small (solvable in 4–10 moves), reset them when the player leaves the room, and never allow a soft-lock. Horror version: the statue moves one tile on its own whenever the player turns away. *Why it works:* Short spatial breaks between the logic and code puzzles change the pace. Resetting prevents the 'I'm stuck forever' failure.
- **Timed escape set pieces**: Used for climaxes: a visible or implied timer (fire spreading, walls closing, the monster breaking a door), with a route the player has already walked or that is clearly marked. Keep them to about 30–90 seconds, with a checkpoint just before. The Witch's House's final chase by a legless figure crawling after you, and corridors whose walls close in, are well-remembered examples (from memory). *Why it works:* The player gets a burst of adrenaline after long quiet stretches. Because thinking is minimal under time pressure, the fear doesn't turn into frustration.
- **Adjustable puzzle difficulty and hints**: Silent Hill 2 and 3 let you set riddle difficulty separately from combat. The hint text for the same puzzle (for example SH2's coin puzzle) changes by level (from memory). For a browser game: a protagonist's notebook that updates the current objectives with two or three hints that get gradually more direct, unlocked after some time or on request. *Why it works:* It lets the 1–2 hour runtime hold for casual players without making the puzzles less satisfying for experienced ones.
- **Hidden flags that decide the ending**: Ib, Mad Father and The Witch's House all have several endings decided by optional actions: reading certain diaries, being kind to or ignoring a companion, finding hidden objects. The true ending in these games usually needs thorough exploration (from memory: in The Witch's House, reading a particular diary affects the ending). *Why it works:* Optional puzzles and story notes become meaningful, which rewards careful exploration and gives a reason to replay.
- **Health as a physical object**: Ib represents health as a rose. Petals fall when you are hurt, and putting the rose in a vase of water restores it. Variant: a candle that burns down, a lantern with oil, or a doll that loses stitches. *Why it works:* The resource is part of the story and the art, so the HUD can stay minimal, and losing health feels more personal than a number.

### Puzzle examples

- (Resident Evil (1996) — from memory, unverified details) The mansion has doors marked with crest symbols that need matching keys. A piano can play a specific piece from found sheet music, which opens a hidden room with an emblem. The emblem connects to an object in another room (a swap between a real and a decoy emblem). *Lesson:* A hub with locked, symbol-coded wings, plus puzzles that span several rooms (piano → hidden room → object elsewhere), makes the house feel like one machine. Use symbols the player can read at a glance.
- (Silent Hill (1999) — from memory) A piano in the school must be played using a poem about birds. Each bird line maps to a white or black key, and 'silent' birds mark keys that make no sound. *Lesson:* A self-contained, poetic clue fits the horror tone and can still be solved by careful reading. The answer can be checked against the poem, so there's no guesswork.
- (Silent Hill 2 (2001) — from memory) A coin-placement puzzle whose hint poem changes with the separately chosen 'riddle level'. *Lesson:* Offer adjustable puzzle difficulty or layered hints so the runtime holds for different kinds of players.
- (Ib (kouri, 2012) — from memory) Painting titles and gallery plaques work as hints. One room is a logic puzzle where several figures make statements and the player must work out who is lying or truthful to choose the right one. Health is a rose restored at vases. *Lesson:* Use the setting's own medium (art in a gallery) as the hint system. Pure logic puzzles fit horror when every piece of information is visible on screen at once.
- (The Witch's House (Fummy, 2012) — from memory) The house leaves written messages and taunts. Touching the wrong thing often kills you instantly (walls, crushing, creatures). A black cat is the save point, and a late sequence is a timed chase through earlier rooms. *Lesson:* Instant deaths are fine only with warnings, frequent saves and instant retries. The building can be the antagonist and give its own clues. Reuse known rooms for the final chase so the player already knows the route.
- (Ao Oni (noprops, 2004) — general mechanics from memory) A mansion made mostly from default RPG Maker tiles. Picking up key items or entering certain rooms makes the monster appear and chase; closets let you hide; keys for one area are found in another, so you go back through rooms where it has appeared. *Lesson:* Tie threat to progress (pickups trigger danger) and place backtracking routes where the monster has appeared before. Anticipating the monster does most of the scaring.
- (Mad Father (Sen, 2012, Wolf RPG Editor) — from memory) The protagonist gains a tool (a chainsaw) that clears doll and obstacle blockers. Deaths unlock illustrated dead-end scenes, and ending flags depend on optional actions. *Lesson:* A tool that works as a key for a whole category of obstacles lets the game re-gate areas the player has already seen. Illustrated deaths reward curiosity instead of only punishing it.
- (OneShot (2016) — from memory) A password needed in-game is written to a text file in the player's real Documents folder. *Lesson:* Clues that break the fourth wall make a memorable signature puzzle. In a browser the closest version is the tab title or something like a 'print this page' gimmick. Use at most one, and signpost it heavily.
- (Myst (1993) — from memory) Library books and journals hold the backstory and also the clues for machines found elsewhere. Marker switches act as a checklist of places to visit. *Lesson:* Putting clues inside the lore (diaries, letters) gives every piece of story text a mechanical purpose, so players read it closely. That suits slowly revealed psychological horror.
- (Gabriel Knight 3 (1999) — negative example, from memory) The infamous 'cat hair mustache' sequence: the player builds a fake mustache from tape and cat hair to impersonate a man who doesn't even have a mustache. *Lesson:* This is the textbook case of moon logic. When a solution's chain of reasoning can't be predicted from in-world motivation and clues, players turn to walkthroughs and the horror stops working.
- (Monkey Island 2 (1991) — negative example, from memory) The 'monkey wrench' puzzle depends on an English pun (using a monkey as a wrench). *Lesson:* Avoid solutions that depend on wordplay, especially if the game may be translated. Base solutions on physical or story logic the player can see.
- (Template for the designer (original pattern, not from any game)) The hub's piano is missing three keys. A child's music box in wing A plays a 5-note tune. A torn page in wing B labels the notes by color. The keys are hidden in three rooms, and taking the last one triggers a chase back to the hub. *Lesson:* Shows the lock before the key, a clue split across two wings with some redundancy, and a pickup that triggers a chase. A single puzzle ties together exploration, deduction and a scare.

### Atmosphere and pacing

- Alternate thinking and fleeing: rooms with hard puzzles should be calm (or only threatened while you walk in), and chases should need movement, not deduction. Mixing the two turns fear into frustration.
- Danger tied to progress (Ao Oni style): pickups, certain doors and returning to solved rooms are the trigger points. Players start to dread their own actions, so tension stays up without constant scares.
- Rough pacing estimate (my estimate, not from a source): for a 1–2 hour game, plan about 10–15 real puzzles (needing deduction or spatial thinking) plus 15–25 smaller lock/key/item steps, with a chase or major scare about every 8–15 minutes and a quieter stretch after each.
- Safe-room rhythm: a save point (Witch's House black cat style) and the hub work as recovery spaces. Make the player feel that 'safe' is temporary by having the safe space disturbed once in the late game.
- Text pacing: RPG Maker horror delivers dread through short lines in text boxes, pauses ('...'), portrait expression changes and silence before a stinger. Silence and a single sudden sound effect are stronger than constant music.
- Darkness and a small view radius make rooms feel bigger and hide threats. Ration jump scares (perhaps 1 major one per 15–20 minutes); most of the fear should come from anticipation.
- Changing the world on revisit (new notes, moved objects, lights off) keeps backtracking scary and shows that the house watches the player.
- Escalate per wing: the first wing teaches the verbs with low threat, the middle wings add the pursuer and trap rooms, and the last act combines puzzles under a final timed escape.

### Story structure

- Common setup in the genre: a small cast enters an isolated place (mansion, gallery, forest house) on a dare, by accident or because they're trapped. The exit locks immediately, which sets the overall goal ('escape').
- The backstory comes out in fragments found in the environment (diaries, letters, research notes, children's drawings) that also carry puzzle clues. Reading is both how the story is told and how the game is played.
- The twist reframes the protagonist or companion: Ib's companion turns out to be a painting; The Witch's House reveals an identity swap; Mad Father reveals the father's experiments and the protagonist's own dark inheritance (from memory). The psychological horror usually lies in what the protagonist is or did, not only in the monster.
- Companions are emotional anchors who can be lost (Ao Oni's friends are picked off; Ib's Garry can be saved or lost by choices). Their fate gives weight to optional puzzles.
- Several endings decided by hidden flags (optional diaries read, kindness shown, items found), usually a bad, a normal and a true ending. The true ending rewards thorough exploration.
- Recommended original structure: a prologue that teaches the controls → a hub with 3–4 wings, each revealing one layer of the backstory → a middle twist that changes the hub's state → a final wing and timed escape → an ending that depends on flags.
- Keep the story and characters original. Borrow structure (a hub, fragmented diaries, a twist about identity, several endings), never names, lines or specific puzzle solutions.

### Art and audio

- The classic look is RPG Maker top-down/3-4 view: 32×32 tiles (XP/VX era; 2000/2003 used 16×16 scaled up), small chibi sprites 2–3 heads tall, and 4-direction walk cycles of 3 frames.
- Ao Oni is remembered for building atmosphere mostly from default RPG Maker tiles plus one strange, oversized monster sprite (from memory). Contrast and a distinctive threat design matter more than custom tiles.
- Bust portraits in the dialogue box with several expressions (calm, scared, crying, shocked) carry a lot of the emotion. Separate full-screen still images mark key scares and deaths (Mad Father's illustrated dead ends, from memory).
- Muted palettes with one accent color tied to the theme (Ib's red rose and blue in the gallery; Ao Oni's blue creature against beige rooms). Restrict saturated red to blood and danger so it keeps its impact.
- Lighting: a dark overlay with a soft circle of light around the player, flickering lamps, and colored light (a blue moon through windows) to set the mood.
- Audio: mostly silence or a low ambient drone (wind, clock ticking, a hum). Piano and music-box motifs are genre staples. Door creak, footstep and item-pickup sounds give feedback. A distinct chase loop plays when the pursuer appears and a short stinger marks reveals, so players learn to fear those cues.
- Puzzle sounds: a satisfying click or chime for correct steps (combination dials, piano notes), and a dull thud or text for wrong ones. Audio confirmation lowers frustration.

### Criticisms and pitfalls

- Trial-and-error instant deaths (often said about The Witch's House): fine with warnings and instant retries, cheap without them. Never place a save point where it can trap the player in an unwinnable state.
- Obscure or moon-logic puzzles send players to walkthroughs, which breaks immersion and fear. Gilbert's rules: clear goals, no backwards puzzles, reward intent, no dead ends.
- Dead ends and soft-locks: consumable items used wrongly, push blocks stuck in corners, missed items in areas that later close (Gilbert's 'I forgot to pick it up'). Make key items impossible to lose and puzzles resettable.
- Pixel hunting: interactables with no visual difference. Use glints or distinctive sprites.
- Dull lock-and-key fetching: finding key after key with no deduction. Mix in logic, code, spatial and sound puzzles, and make the key's location a small puzzle in itself.
- Backtracking boredom: long walks with nothing changed. Add shortcuts and changes of state, and move the pursuer.
- Unfair or buggy chase AI: monsters that get stuck, block doors or move unpredictably fast. Make the pursuer slightly slower than the player in open corridors, and make every chase escapable with good routing.
- Long unskippable cutscenes or dialogue replayed after each death. Let players skip or speed them up, and checkpoint after cutscenes.
- Red herrings in horror waste the player's limited patience. Use at most a few, and give them story value.
- Puzzles that need careful thought while the monster is chasing you (fear turns into frustration).
- Copying famous puzzle solutions (the bird-poem piano, the liars room) makes the game feel derivative. Reuse the structure with different content and logic.

### Playtime and scope

These playtimes are from memory and approximate, not checked against HowLongToBeat: The Witch's House is roughly 1–2 hours for a first ending, Ao Oni roughly 1–2 hours, Ib roughly 2–3 hours for one ending, and Mad Father roughly 2–3 hours. Suggested scope for an original 1–2 hour browser game: a prologue (5–10 min) + a hub + 3–4 wings (15–25 min each) + a finale with a timed escape (10 min). That comes to about 25–40 small rooms (many single screens), about 10–15 real puzzles, 15–25 smaller lock/key/item steps, 3–6 chases, 1 pursuer type plus 1–2 local threats, 2–4 endings decided by 3–6 hidden flags, an optional hint notebook, and save points about every 10–15 minutes. Before building, draw the puzzle dependency chart backwards from the ending and keep 2–3 open threads available at any time.

## How horror is built in low-fi 2D games (RPG Maker-style): chases, scares, sound, darkness, uncanny imagery, environmental storytelling, unreliable narrators, psychological themes, and why primitive graphics can be scarier

The content's thesis: low-fi 2D horror works by holding back information, mostly through abstraction, darkness, silence and off-screen events, and letting the player's imagination fill the gaps (Radcliffe and Stephen King's 'terror > horror > revulsion'; Scott McCloud's 'amplification through simplification' and 'closure'). It then uses rare, sharply timed shocks (a chase sting, a sudden high-detail image, a one-touch death) against that quiet baseline. The classics (Ao Oni, The Witch's House, Ib, Mad Father, Yume Nikki, Corpse Party) wrap a simple explore-find-unlock puzzle loop in a constant threat: a pursuer that can appear during routine searching, deaths that teach, a diegetic health system, and a story told through diaries and room details that ends by recasting who the player really was (guilt, family, memory, identity).


### Mechanics

- **Telegraphed chaser reveal (the 'reveal beat')**: In Ao Oni-style chases, the pursuer appears at a doorway or room edge with a sudden music change (chase theme or sting). Recommended implementation: when it spawns, play a sting, freeze the creature for about 0.6–1.0 s (a 'reveal beat' that lets the player see it and turn), then let it move toward the player. Never spawn it within about 4 tiles of the player or between the player and every exit. Use chase music as the 'you are being hunted' state and cut it to silence when the creature loses the player. *Why it works:* The sting and pause make the scare land (shock) while keeping it fair: the player always gets a beat to react, so a death feels like the player's mistake, not the game's. Starting and stopping the chase music gives the player a clear 'danger on / danger off' signal, and the drop back to silence is itself unnerving.
- **Scripted + semi-random spawns run by a tension 'director'**: Ao Oni mixes scripted appearances (on story beats, opening certain doors, picking up key items) with the threat of appearing when you enter or leave rooms (exact random rules per version: verify). Implementation pattern borrowed from Left 4 Dead's AI Director (Michael Booth's 'adaptive dramatic pacing': build-up → sustained peak → peak fade → relax): keep a tension value; after every chase or big scare enforce a cooldown (e.g., 60–120 s or 3+ room transitions) with spawn chance 0. After that, each room transition rolls a chance that rises slowly (e.g., 3% → 15%). Never spawn while a puzzle UI, note or dialogue is open, or in a save room. *Why it works:* Purely scripted chases become predictable and purely random ones feel cheap. A director creates real uncertainty ('it could come any time') while guaranteeing breathing room, so dread builds between shocks. The cooldown stops players from getting used to it.
- **Fair escape geometry (loops, doors, chokepoints)**: Rooms where chases can happen should have at least two exits or a loop (around a table, around a block of rooms) so the player can kite the creature. Chaser speed should be about 90–100% of the player's; slightly slower on corners or doors so skilled play gets rewarded. Doors and stairs act as breakpoints: the creature follows through after a delay (e.g., 1–2 s). After N transitions without being seen, or a timeout (e.g., 20–30 s), the chase ends. *Why it works:* Fear lasts only while the player believes escape is possible but uncertain. A dead-end trap with no telegraph makes the player feel helpless in a frustrating way, not a scary one. A loop gives the player agency and the panic of 'is it still behind me?'
- **Hiding spots (closets/wardrobes, under beds)**: Ao Oni popularized hiding in closets during chases. Interacting with a hiding spot hides the player sprite. Line of sight breaks, and the creature walks to the player's last known position, waits or searches for a few seconds, then leaves. Recommended: show the room from the hiding spot with a dark vignette and audible footsteps. To keep hiding tense, give a small chance the creature checks a spot it SAW the player enter, but never one it didn't see. *Why it works:* Hiding turns flight into a waiting game, and the player's only information is sound. Rules must be readable ('if it didn't see me go in, I'm safe') so the tension comes from timing and doubt, not randomness.
- **Diegetic health: the rose (Ib)**: In Ib, the player's life is a rose. Damage (hands lunging from walls, creatures touching you) removes petals, vases of water scattered around restore it, and losing all petals means death. Companions have their own roses, which the story can threaten. *Why it works:* Health becomes a fragile, beautiful object in the fiction, not a HUD bar, so taking damage feels like losing part of yourself. Ib also uses the rose as a story device: threatening a companion's rose makes the stakes personal. Cheap to build in pixel art (one icon with N states).
- **One-touch deaths + generous saves (The Witch's House)**: In The Witch's House, almost anything can kill the protagonist instantly: walls closing in, things grabbing her, traps triggered by taking an obvious item or reading the wrong thing. A black cat acts as the save point and is placed often. Recommended: deaths show a short death message or tableau, then offer an instant reload of the last save or an auto checkpoint at room entry. *Why it works:* Deaths are surprising, often darkly funny punchlines, and they make every interaction feel risky ('should I touch this?'), which is dread. Frequent saves keep it from becoming punishment. Each death teaches a rule of the house, so death works as information.
- **Darkness and limited light**: Draw the map dark and give the player a small light radius (lamp or candle), or use 'lights-out' events where the screen goes dark for a moment and, when the lights return, statues, dolls or mannequins have moved closer (Ib uses moving statues and dolls; check exact implementation). Rule: hostile objects only move while unobserved (lights off or off-screen). *Why it works:* Darkness hides information, so the imagination supplies threats. The 'moves when you can't see it' rule makes the player complicit: you have to stop watching to make progress (walking, reading), so normal actions become frightening.
- **Sound design: silence, ambience, stings, warning cues**: Use silence or near-silence (room tone, a clock, wind) as the baseline in exploration rooms. Save music for safe rooms (soft melody), chases (aggressive loop), and story beats. Use stings (one sharp hit or distorted sound) only for reveals. Add audio warnings before danger, like Silent Hill's radio, which crackles with static when monsters are near: footsteps in the next room, a door creaking off-screen, a distant thud. *Why it works:* Fear works on contrast. If music plays all the time, stings lose their force. Off-screen sound is the cheapest scare in a 2D game: the player pictures the source, which is scarier than any sprite. Warning cues create anticipation, the 'bomb under the table' (Hitchcock: showing the bomb creates suspense, hiding it gives only a short surprise).
- **Rare randomized scare in a 'safe' place**: Yume Nikki's Uboa: in the Number World, flipping a light switch normally just toggles the lights, but occasionally the face on the wall is replaced by the distorted Uboa face with a harsh sound. Touching it traps the player in darkness (exact odds: verify). Pattern: put a very low-probability alternate outcome on a harmless, repeatable interaction. *Why it works:* It breaks the player's sense that a learned space is safe. Because it's rare, players doubt what they saw and tell each other about it, which spreads the game's myth.
- **Fragmented documents (diaries, notes, name tags)**: The Witch's House scatters pages of the witch's diary through the house. Corpse Party has players collect the name tags of dead students, each with a final memory. Recommended: write 10–20 short fragments (40–120 words). Order them so early ones seem to be about one person and late ones show they're about someone else. Some fragments double as puzzle clues (a date = a safe code). *Why it works:* The player builds the story themselves (active storytelling), and the pacing stays in the designer's hands. Making the same fragment carry both a clue and lore rewards reading and avoids text dumps.
- **Unreliable protagonist / identity reveal**: The Witch's House ending reveals that the girl you played is actually the witch in a stolen body, and the legless 'monster' chasing you in the final scene is the real girl. Omori reveals the protagonist has repressed a traumatic act, recovered through a photo album and memory fragments. Implementation: plant 5+ fair clues, such as NPC or creature reactions that make more sense once you know, a reflection or portrait that doesn't match, a diary detail only the 'villain' would know, and a pursuer that seems oddly pitiful. *Why it works:* It turns the whole playthrough into evidence and creates a second, guilt-based horror ('I was the monster'). A replay or reflection looks different afterward, which adds length and word of mouth.
- **Uncanny objects and 'familiar made strange'**: Dolls, mannequins, portraits, stuffed animals, a parent who is subtly wrong, a familiar room revisited with one detail changed (a portrait's eyes closed, a toy moved, text now saying 'COME BACK'). Ib and Mad Father both use dolls and artworks as threats. Freud's 'The Uncanny' (1919) points to dolls and automata and doubles, and Mori's 'uncanny valley' (1970) to near-human figures. *Why it works:* Things that are almost human, or familiar but wrong, create unease without gore. Backtracking stops being boring because every revisit might show a change.
- **Multiple endings from hidden flags**: Ib has several endings that depend on how the player treats companions and on choices and actions during play (details: verify). The Witch's House adds a true ending/extra content on a repeat playthrough. Recommended: 3 endings (bad / normal / true), driven by 3–5 hidden flags (helped a companion, read all diary pages, a mercy choice at the climax). *Why it works:* Replay can double a 1-hour game's length, and the flags reinforce the themes (empathy, guilt) by making the player's moral behavior decide the ending.
- **Meta / fourth-wall puzzles**: OneShot (RPG Maker XP) puts a computer password in a file it writes to the player's real computer, and uses the game window itself in puzzles. Browser equivalents: change document.title when the tab is unfocused ('don't leave me'), hide a code in the page title, or use the system clock or date. *Why it works:* Breaks the safe frame of 'it's just a game' for a memorable spike of unease. Use it once or twice, never as a requirement the player can't find without hints.
- **Safe rooms and save points**: A save object (Witch's House black cat; journals or books in other games) in a lit room with gentle music and no spawns. *Why it works:* Creates the 'relax' phase of the pacing curve. The contrast makes leaving the room scary. Breaking the rule once, late in the game (the safe room is violated), is a powerful escalation beat.

### Puzzle examples

- (Ao Oni (noprops, 2008, RPG Maker XP)) A mansion of locked doors: a key or tool from one room opens another, and codes and clues are scattered across rooms. The blue pursuer can appear while you are doing routine searching or walking between rooms (scripted on certain doors and pickups, and possibly at random depending on version; verify). Closets give places to hide. *Lesson:* The puzzles are simple lock-and-key and code chains. The horror comes from making backtracking dangerous. Design the puzzle map so solving requires crossing 'hunting grounds' and returning to rooms already explored.
- (The Witch's House (Fummy, 2012, RPG Maker VX)) Rooms where the obvious action (take the item, read the note, go closer) triggers an instant-death trap. The safe solution comes from a hint in the room or on a note, or from a second item that disarms the trap. A black cat saves the game frequently. *Lesson:* Put the clue in the same screen as the trap so death feels fair in hindsight. Pair lethal traps with saves less than about 60 seconds apart. Make each death message short and in character.
- (The Witch's House (Fummy, 2012)) Final escape: after the reveal, the legless 'witch' crawls quickly after the player through the house's rooms to the exit, while new obstacles appear on the route back. *Lesson:* A climactic chase that reuses a known layout under pressure turns map knowledge into the skill being tested. The pursuer's identity (revealed in the ending) makes it tragic as well as scary.
- (Ib (kouri, 2012, RPG Maker 2000)) Gallery puzzles driven by paintings and sculptures: corridors where hands reach from the walls (lost rose petals), a 'Lady' painting that crawls out of its frame and chases, statues that move during lights-out. One room makes the player work out which of several talking figures is truthful (players call it a 'liars' puzzle; verify details). *Lesson:* One theme (an art gallery) can produce every puzzle and threat. The setting's own objects make the rules, so puzzles feel natural, not bolted on. A deduction or logic puzzle gives a calm, thoughtful change of pace between chases.
- (Yume Nikki (Kikiyama, 2004, RPG Maker 2003)) Collect 'effects' (transformations and items) hidden across dream worlds; some effects open access to new areas or events. There is no dialogue, no explicit objectives, and the ending only comes after gathering them all. *Lesson:* Exploration itself can be the puzzle. Ambiguity (no explanation) produces dread and theorizing. For a 1–2 hour game, borrow just a small 'collect N memories to unlock the final door' structure, with clear progress feedback.
- (Corpse Party (Team GrisGris, original 1996)) Find the name tags of dead students in a haunted school; each one plays that victim's last moments. *Lesson:* A collectible that is also a short death story makes environmental storytelling into a loop of reward and dread. It works well as an optional side hunt that affects the true ending.
- (OneShot (Future Cat, 2016, RPG Maker XP)) A password needed in-game is in a file the game creates on the player's real computer. Other puzzles involve the game window itself. *Lesson:* One meta puzzle per game makes a memorable spike. In a browser, use the tab title, URL hash, or the real date and time, and always provide an in-game fallback hint.
- (Omori (Omocat, 2020, RPG Maker MV)) The truth is pieced together through a photo album with missing or altered photos and memory fragments in an inner world, leading up to the protagonist confronting a repressed act. *Lesson:* Memory as missing pieces maps cleanly onto puzzle collection: each recovered photo or page is a puzzle reward and also a step toward the guilt reveal.

### Atmosphere and pacing

- Use a sine-wave pacing curve (build-up → peak → fade → relax, after Left 4 Dead's director): each 10–15 min 'chapter' should hold a quiet exploration stretch, 2–3 small unsettling events, one major scare or chase, then a safe room.
- Dread (anticipation, uncertainty) does more work than shock. Following Hitchcock's 'bomb under the table', show or hint at the threat early (a sound behind a door, scratches on a closet) and make the player wait. Pay off maybe 1 in 3 setups with an actual scare so players can't predict which.
- Keep silence as the baseline. Ambience (clock ticks, wind, house creaks) at low volume. Music only in safe rooms, chases and story beats. A sudden music cut-out is itself a warning.
- Space out jump scares: roughly 1 major shock per 8–12 minutes. Pair each with a reveal beat (freeze frame, pause, sting) and a short calm afterward. Never stack two shocks back to back, or the second does nothing.
- Escalate by changing the familiar: revisit early rooms later with something altered (moved furniture, new writing on a wall, a door now locked from the other side, a portrait facing away). Cheap in tile-based maps and very effective.
- Make the first scare 'nothing': in the first 5 minutes, set up an expected scare that doesn't happen (an open closet that's empty) to teach players the game will play with expectations.
- Trap the player within ~5 minutes: a mundane opening (going home, visiting a gallery, a dare to enter a house), then a door locks, a path is blocked, a parent vanishes. The rule 'you cannot leave until…' gives the whole game its goal.
- Darkness should vary: well-lit safe rooms, dim exploration rooms, pitch-black 'lantern-only' rooms for set pieces. Changes in light tell the player how much danger to expect.
- Off-screen events beat on-screen ones in 2D: a scream from another room, a thud, then the companion is missing. Show the aftermath (a blood tile, a dropped item) rather than the act.
- Avoid putting a threat during long reading or puzzle input. Let the player fully think, then put the danger on the route to where the answer is used.

### Story structure

- Archetype: a child or teen protagonist trapped in one bounded place (a mansion, a gallery, the family house, a school) that stands for a psychological state. The place's rules are the story's rules.
- Three acts in about 60–90 min: Act 1 (0–20 min) entrapment, learn the rules, first chase; Act 2 (20–60 min) expanding map, companion(s), lore fragments, escalating threats, midpoint loss (a companion vanishes or dies, or a safe room is violated); Act 3 (60–90 min) reveal, final chase or confrontation, branching endings.
- Family themes: Mad Father's protagonist Aya loves a father who performs horrific experiments, and the story asks how much she knew or accepted (details of the true ending: verify). Horror comes from loving the monster: ask 'what did the child see and choose not to know?'
- Identity: The Witch's House's body-swap twist recasts the player character as the villain and the 'monster' as the victim. Plant evidence throughout (diary entries, how creatures treat you, a pitiful pursuer) so the twist rewards attention rather than coming from nowhere.
- Guilt and repressed memory (Omori pattern): the inner world or dream is a defense against a memory. Puzzles 'recover' memories, and the final area is the memory itself. Endings depend on whether the protagonist accepts the truth.
- Companion structure (Ib's Ib, Garry and Mary): a kind companion builds attachment; an ambiguous companion who turns out to be something else creates betrayal. Endings depend on how the player treated each.
- Tell the story through environment and fragments, not cutscenes: diaries, drawings, labels, letters, room arrangement (a nursery with one crib removed). Keep total text short. Each fragment should raise a question or answer one.
- Endings: a 'bad' ending (trapped or replaced) for missed flags, a 'normal' escape with unresolved guilt, a 'true' ending that needs optional lore and a compassionate choice. A short post-credits image is enough to haunt.
- Original-concept warning for the designer: avoid reusing the specific premises (blue monster in a mansion with friends; witch body-swap; living gallery with roses; doll-making father). Borrow structures, not content.

### Art and audio

- RPG Maker technical baseline (prior knowledge): RPG Maker 2000/2003 used 16×16 tiles at 320×240; XP used 32×32 at 640×480; VX/VX Ace 32×32 at 544×416. Character sprites typically have 4 facing directions × 3 walk frames. A 3/4 top-down view with chunky outlines matches this look.
- Limited, desaturated palettes with one meaningful accent color: Ib's red rose against grey gallery walls; Ao Oni's blue monster against brown wood interiors. Make your accent color the color of your threat or theme.
- Why primitive graphics can be scarier: (1) closure: the viewer fills gaps between few pixels or off-screen events with their own worst image (McCloud, Understanding Comics); (2) amplification through simplification: simple, iconic avatars get more player projection and identification; (3) terror over horror: what is half-seen frightens more than what is fully shown (Ann Radcliffe; Stephen King's Danse Macabre hierarchy terror > horror > gross-out); (4) limitation as atmosphere: Silent Hill's fog was widely reported to hide hardware draw-distance limits and became its signature; (5) crude 'wrongness': a creature with off proportions (huge head, stiff motion, a fixed grin) in an otherwise neat world reads as uncanny; (6) pareidolia: a few dark pixels in a portrait read as eyes.
- Contrast technique: keep map sprites small and cute or neutral, then show a rare high-detail illustration (face close-up, death tableau, CG event image) for key moments. The jump in detail is itself the shock. Use under 10 per game.
- Animation for creepiness: unnaturally fast or jerky movement (skip frames), creatures that face the player no matter which way they move, a sprite that doesn't animate while moving (slides), or one extra frame of a smile.
- Screen effects: a brief red flash plus shake on damage; screen tint shifts (cold blue for safe, sick green or red for danger); a vignette that tightens during chases; occasional single-frame glitches. Use sparingly.
- Text as a visual: messages written on walls or floors in the tile layer ('GO BACK', repeated words); text boxes that type slower, change color or show a different speaker name for unsettling lines.
- Audio palette: room-tone loops per area (hum, clock, rain); diegetic sounds (doors, footsteps on wood vs. tile, a dripping tap); 3–5 stings (piano cluster, reversed cymbal, high violin screech, low thump); a chase loop with a driving rhythm; a fragile safe-room melody (music box or solo piano). WebAudio can synthesize most of these: filtered noise for wind and static, detuned oscillators for drones.
- Positional audio for telegraphing: pan and attenuate creature footsteps by distance and direction so players can 'hear it in the next room'. Static or crackle that rises as the creature gets closer (after Silent Hill's radio) is a cheap, readable proximity meter.

### Criticisms and pitfalls

- NOTE: no sources could be read in this session (search budget used up; network policy blocked Wikipedia, Fandom, TV Tropes, gamedeveloper.com). These pitfalls come from prior knowledge of common player and reviewer complaints about the genre and should be checked if possible.
- Trial-and-error deaths without saves nearby feel like punishment. The Witch's House offsets its instant deaths with frequent cat saves; copy the safety net, not just the deaths.
- Familiarity kills fear: once players learn a chaser's exact rules (speed, door delay, despawn), it becomes a nuisance. Keep fear going by varying the pattern (new spawn locations, a second threat type, a safe room violated once), and reveal the creature less as the game goes on.
- Chases that interrupt puzzle-solving or reading are maddening, not scary. Protect the thinking moments.
- Unfair spawns (on top of the player, in dead ends, right after a save load) lead players to quit. Enforce minimum spawn distance and a grace period after loading.
- Moon logic: puzzles whose solution relies on a leap the game never hinted at. Every lock needs a clue the player can find before or near it. Avoid 'use every item on everything'.
- Too much backtracking across a large map with no shortcuts pads time without tension. Open shortcuts as the map grows.
- Hidden ending flags that are too obscure send players to guides (a common complaint about multi-ending RPG Maker games). Give in-world hints that kindness or reading matters.
- Overused jump scares become predictable and annoying. Pace them out and mix in false alarms and slow-burn moments.
- Hitbox ambiguity in tile-based chases: diagonal or partial-tile collisions feel unfair. Use tile-based or generous hitboxes for catches (catch only when in the same tile or overlapping by more than 50%).
- Text-heavy exposition dumps break dread. Keep notes short and let rooms tell the story.
- Gore or shock without a theme feels cheap. The classics are remembered for their tragic reveals (identity, family, guilt), not their deaths.
- Accessibility: give players a volume control, a flashing-effects toggle, and maybe a 'calm mode' (slower chaser) so more people finish the story.

### Playtime and scope

From memory (unverified this session): The Witch's House is commonly cited at about 1–2 hours for a first run. Ib takes about 1.5–2 hours per playthrough, with multiple endings encouraging replays. Ao Oni runs about 1–2 hours depending on version. Mad Father takes about 2–3 hours. Recommended scope for a 1–2 hour original browser game: one main location (a house or building with 3–4 zones or floors plus a final area), about 25–40 small rooms (each 1–2 screens), 15–25 puzzles (mostly lock-and-key and code chains, plus 3–5 'set-piece' puzzles: logic or deduction, sequence or pattern, light/dark, statue-moving, item combination), 1 main pursuer with 4–6 scripted chases plus director-driven random spawns, 2–3 special threats (crawling painting or doll, lights-out statues, hands in walls), 10–20 lore fragments, 3 endings (bad / normal / true) from 3–5 hidden flags, and about 6–10 high-detail scare or story illustrations. Count roughly 3–5 minutes of play per puzzle including exploration, plus chases, deaths and reading, to reach 60–90 min on a first run, and more with replay for the true ending.

### Mechanics

- **Tile grid and wall-height convention (unverified, from prior knowledge, high confidence)**: RPG Maker 2000/2003: 320x240 screen, 16x16 tiles, one 'chipset' image of 480x256 holding all lower and upper layer tiles, with animated water and autotiles in the top-left block. RPG Maker XP: 640x480, 32x32 tiles, tileset image 256px wide (8 tiles) with any height, autotiles 96x128 strips. VX/VX Ace: 544x416, 32x32 tiles, A1-A5 autotile/floor/wall sheets plus B-E object sheets. WOLF RPG Editor (used for The Witch's House, Mad Father and The Crooked Man) defaults to 16x16 tiles at 320x240. In all of them, interior rooms follow the same 3/4 convention: a dark 'ceiling/wall-top' band, then a wall face 2 tiles tall (sometimes 3 in large halls), then floor. Doors, windows, paintings and clocks sit on the wall face. Tall furniture (bookshelves, wardrobes) stands on the first floor row and its sprite overlaps the wall face. Rooms are often smaller than the screen and centred on black. *Why it works:* The flat, frontal wall face is a canvas for horror decals: writing that appears, hand prints, eyes, paintings that change. The ceiling band hides what's 'above'. Small rooms floating on black make the space feel isolated and stage-like. For this project: 16x16 tiles at 416x240 gives a 26x15-tile view, the same density as RM2000/WOLF games, so the mapping habits carry over directly.
- **Character sprite sheets and 3-frame walk (unverified, high confidence)**: RM2000/2003 charset: 288x256 sheet = 4x2 characters, each 3 frames x 4 directions of 24x32 px, rows ordered up, right, down, left. VX/Ace: 32x32 frames, 3x4 per character, 8 characters per 384x256 sheet; a '$' filename prefix means one character per sheet, and a '!' prefix marks objects drawn without the default 4px upward offset. XP instead uses 4 frames x 4 directions per file (commonly 32x48 frames), rows down, left, right, up. The 3-frame walk plays as 1-2-3-2: left step, stand (middle column), right step, stand. Proportions are chibi: about 2 to 2.5 heads tall in RM2000/VX (head roughly 40-50% of sprite height), with large dark eyes as 2-3 px clusters, a 1 px dark outline, and 2-3 shades per material. XP RTP characters are taller, about 3-4 heads. *Why it works:* Chibi protagonists read as small, young and vulnerable (Ib is a 9-year-old girl, Viola a young girl, Aya a child). Their cuteness is what monsters or gore contrast against. For this project: 16x24 or 24x32 frames on 16px tiles (RM2000 scale). Draw idle 'emotion poses' as extra frames on the same sheet: kneel, fall, look up, shiver, hands up in fright. These games reuse such poses for cutscenes instead of drawing new art.
- **Face portraits and emotion sets (unverified, high confidence on formats)**: RM2000 faceset: 192x192 image = 4x4 grid of 48x48 faces. VX/Ace: 96x96 faces, 8 per 384x192 sheet (4x2). XP had no default faces; Ao Oni (made in XP) mostly runs without portraits. Each sheet is used as one character's emotion range: neutral, smile, surprised, sad, scared, crying, angry or serious, and a special one (a blank stare or a hurt face). The face sits at the left of the message window; text wraps to its right. *Why it works:* Faces carry characterisation the tiny sprites cannot. A 'scared' face on the right line lands a scare even when the map shows nothing. Withholding a portrait (an unseen speaker, or a portrait that is only a silhouette) is itself a dread tool. For this project: 48x48 faces (or 40x40 at 240px height) beside a 4-line box about 64-76 px tall. Budget about 6-8 emotions for the lead, 3-4 for side characters, and 1-2 'wrong' variants (eyes too wide, smile too big) for the reveal of a corrupted character.
- **Monster design by scale and style contrast (unverified, medium confidence)**: Ao Oni's Oni is a hairless, smooth blue/purple humanoid with an oversized head and a crude, too-human face (big staring eyes, wide mouth). It is drawn noticeably larger than the 1x2-tile player sprite and in a rougher style than the tidy cast. Later versions add variant Oni with distorted shapes. Ib's threats are gallery art made hostile: women in paintings ('Lady in Red'/'Lady in Blue' style figures) crawling out of their frames across the floor, headless mannequin statues that shift position when unseen or when the lights go out, creepy blue dolls, eyes and grasping arms coming out of walls, and a rose that loses petals. The Witch's House pairs a cute pastel house with sudden gory deaths (for example crushing walls and grabbing hands) and a final crawling, mutilated pursuer. Yume Nikki's Uboa is an ordinary NPC sprite replaced by a pale screaming face during a rare light-switch event. *Why it works:* The monster breaks the rules the rest of the art follows: bigger than the grid, less cute, or a once-safe everyday object (a painting, a doll, a statue, a light switch). Readability at tiny size comes from a simple silhouette and one exaggerated feature (a giant head, a mouth, eyes). Concrete rules for a pixel artist: (1) make the pursuer about 1.5-2x the player's height and at least 2 tiles wide so it fills corridors; (2) give it one asymmetric, slightly 'off' face feature and no outline-smoothing, so it looks wrong next to the clean sprites; (3) draw a full-screen 'scare' portrait of it at about 3-4x resolution for jump-scare frames; (4) make hostile versions of the room's furniture (a painting, a doll, a statue) as alternate sprites of the same object.
- **Diegetic health and status (unverified, high confidence for Ib)**: Ib replaces HP with a rose. The heroine's red rose has a fixed number of petals (5), damage plucks petals, and vases of water restore them. Companions carry their own colour-coded roses (blue, yellow). Losing all petals means death. The menu shows the rose and its petal count rather than numbers. *Why it works:* The health UI is part of the story world and part of the colour-identity system, so the player feels damage as a story event. A single saturated rose colour against muted gallery greys reads instantly. For this project: replace HP with an object (a candle stub, a heart-shaped locket with cracks, a doll with seams) drawn as a 16-24 px menu icon with 3-5 visible damage states.
- **Screen tone, tint and greyscale (unverified, high confidence on commands)**: RM2000 'Tint Screen' takes R, G, B and saturation as 0-200% over N frames. Setting saturation to 0% gives greyscale flashbacks and memories, and dropping R/G gives cold nights. XP/VX/Ace 'Change Screen Color Tone' takes R, G, B (-255..255) and Gray (0..255). As remembered, VX Ace's presets are Normal (0,0,0,0), Dark (-68,-68,-68,0), Sepia (34,-34,-68,170), Sunset (68,-34,-34,0) and Night (-68,-68,0,68). Horror games use tone shifts for 'the world has changed' moments: lights go out, the palette drops toward blue-black, or a red cast appears after a death or a reveal. *Why it works:* A whole-screen colour shift tells the player the rules changed without any new art. The same room repainted by tone (warm, then cold or red) gives a 1-2 hour game more 'locations' cheaply. For this project: E.tint('#300010', a) for blood/reveal states, a blue-violet tint for 'other world' states, and desaturation for memories. Never fade to pure #000: shadows should sit around #120f1e to #1a1530 so silhouettes still read.
- **Flash, shake and full-screen pictures (unverified, high confidence on commands)**: Engine commands used everywhere: 'Flash Screen' (colour, strength, frames), for example white for lightning or red for being hit; 'Shake Screen' (power, speed, frames) for banging doors, slams and the monster entering; 'Show Picture' (image, position, zoom, opacity, with move/rotate/erase) for full-screen scare faces, CG death illustrations, notes and close-ups. A typical jump scare: silence or a quiet ambience, then within 1-3 frames show a large picture, play a loud dissonant sting, shake 6-12 frames, hold about 0.5-1 s, then cut to black or back to the map. Mad Father and The Witch's House are known for special death scenes or illustrations shown on game over (medium confidence). *Why it works:* These commands are cheap, but in moderation they hit hard because the rest of the presentation is still. A white lightning flash can reveal a silhouette for a single flash, which players remember. For this project: E.flash, E.shake, E.scare and E.picture map one-to-one. Rule of thumb: at most one full jump scare per 8-12 minutes. Use 'soft' scares (a shake plus a distant sound, a sprite seen at the edge of the screen for 0.3 s) in between.
- **Darkness and light pools (unverified, medium-high confidence)**: Japanese freeware commonly fakes lighting with a full-screen picture overlay: a black or dark-blue PNG with a soft transparent circle centred on the player, moved with the player each frame. Lamps and candles are extra additive 'glow' pictures. Lighting rooms by switch, candle or flashlight items becomes both a puzzle and an atmosphere tool. *Why it works:* Limited sight radius turns small 26x15-tile rooms into unknown spaces and lets monsters approach from the black. It also hides art reuse. Pitfall: total darkness plus small interactable objects makes pixel-hunting tedious, so give interactables a faint highlight pixel or sparkle. For this project: the engine already has 'dark' and 'light' per room. Keep dark 0.5-0.75 in most rooms and 0.85+ only for set pieces.
- **Layered audio: BGM / BGS / ME / SE and silence (unverified, high confidence on categories)**: From RPG Maker XP on, audio has four channels: BGM (looped music), BGS (looped background sound such as rain, wind, a ticking clock or a fridge hum), ME (one-shot musical jingles such as 'item get' and 'game over') and SE (sound effects). XP accepts MID, OGG, WMA, MP3 and WAV; RM2000 is mostly MIDI and WAV (later versions add MP3). Horror freeware often plays no BGM in exploration rooms, only a BGS bed, saves music for chases, safe rooms and story beats, and uses off-screen SE as information. In Ao Oni, hearing a door open off-screen means the Oni has followed you into the area, and a dedicated chase track starts the moment it appears. *Why it works:* Silence makes every footstep and creak meaningful. A dedicated chase track works as a Pavlovian alarm: the player panics on the first note. Safe-room music (a soft, slightly out-of-tune piano or music box) teaches relief, which makes the next return to silence worse. For this project: give each area a BGS and one BGM at most. Reserve 'chase' as a recognisable motif. Use ME stings for item get and game over. Use positional SE for door, knock and footsteps-behind.
- **Message window and text pacing (unverified, high confidence on RM2000 codes)**: The default is a full-width window at the bottom: 320x80 in RM2000 or the 4-line box in XP/VX, white text on a dark translucent 'windowskin' frame, with an optional face at left. RM2000 control codes shape the pacing: \C[n] colour, \S[n] text speed, \. quarter-second wait, \| one-second wait, \! wait for key press, \> \< instant text, \^ close without input, \N[n] actor name. Horror games use slow speed and waits on '...' lines, colour (usually red) for key words and item names, and boxes with no name label or face for unknown voices. Text that appears on the wall or the map itself (Ib's painted messages) is often a picture or tile change instead of a text box. *Why it works:* Pacing the text controls the player's breathing. A one-second pause before the last word of a line is the cheapest scare tool there is. Moving text out of the UI and onto the environment makes the world itself speak. For this project: add wait tokens to E.say if they are missing, use a red highlight for clue words, and use the '~' shake-line for screams.
- **Scene transitions (unverified, high confidence on RM2000)**: RM2000/2003 'Erase/Show Screen' supports several transition types: fade, dissolve, blinds, vertical and horizontal stripes, mosaic, wave, zoom-type effects and instant cut. XP/VX use a fade or a 'transition graphic' (a greyscale image used as a wipe mask). Horror games use instant cuts for shocks, slow fades for story beats, and mosaic or wave effects for entering the 'other world'. *Why it works:* Transition type tells the player what kind of break this is. An instant cut to black after a scream feels violent; a 2-second fade feels like memory. For this project: an instant cut (0 s), a fast fade (0.25 s) for normal doors, a slow fade (1-2 s) for chapter or story beats, and one special pixel-dissolve for crossing into the nightmare world.

### Puzzle examples

- (Ib (from memory, unverified)) Messages appear painted on the gallery walls and floor as Ib explores. They are written in paint, escalate in tone and sometimes point the way. Gallery placards and painting titles give context and hints for nearby rooms. *Lesson:* Art direction can carry hints. Draw clue text as wall decals or placard sprites rather than dialogue, so solving a puzzle means looking closely at the room. Put the hint on the wall face (the 2-tile W rows) where the player naturally looks.
- (Ib (from memory, unverified)) The rose health system. Hazards (grabbing arms, crawling painted women, traps) pluck petals, and vases of water placed before dangerous stretches restore them. A companion's differently coloured rose can be taken or held hostage as a story and puzzle lever. *Lesson:* Make the resource a visible object with a colour identity. Placing restore points (vases) before danger tells the player what's coming. Drawing damage states (5 to 0 petals) as icon frames makes the threat physical.
- (Ao Oni (from memory, unverified)) Exploration and key or code puzzles happen inside an ordinary mansion while a pursuer can appear at set triggers. The player flees through doors, and the pursuer follows after a short delay (announced by a door sound). The player can hide in closets and similar spots to shake it off. *Lesson:* Hiding spots and chase-relevant doors need clear, readable sprites (a wardrobe with a visible gap, a door with a distinct frame colour). Use sound (a door opening off-screen) as the main warning signal, so audio is part of the puzzle loop.
- (Yume Nikki (from memory, unverified)) An ordinary light switch in a room usually just toggles the lights. Rarely, flipping it replaces a background sprite with a pale screaming face, darkens the room and plays a harsh distorted sound. *Lesson:* An interaction the player trusts is the best place for a scare. Swapping one sprite, changing the screen tone and adding one loud sound costs almost no new art. Randomised or rare versions keep repeat interactions tense.
- (The Witch's House (from memory, medium confidence)) Many interactions in the cute, pastel house can kill the player instantly: picking up or examining the wrong object, or walking somewhere at the wrong moment. Deaths are often hinted beforehand by notes or by a visibly 'wrong' detail, and a talking black cat serves as the save point. *Lesson:* If deaths are instant, put a warning in the art (a crack, a stain, a note) before the trap. Make the save point a recognisable character sprite, not a generic crystal, so 'safe' has a face. Pastel tilesets make sudden red splashes much stronger.
- (Ib (from memory, unverified)) The opening shows a normal, populated art gallery with visitors and placards. At one point the lights flicker, the visitors vanish and the same gallery becomes hostile, with paint messages and moving art. *Lesson:* Reuse one tileset twice: the 'safe' version (NPCs, warm light) and the 'changed' version (empty, tinted, decals added, some sprites swapped). The player sees the change because they already know the space. This saves art for a 1-2 hour scope.

### Atmosphere and pacing

- (All unverified, from prior knowledge.) The baseline is mundane and calm: well-lit ordinary houses (Ao Oni), a clean modern gallery (Ib) or cute pastel rooms (The Witch's House). Horror lands because the art first earns the player's trust.
- Silence or a BGS bed during exploration. Music only for chases, safe rooms and story beats, so music itself signals danger or relief.
- Off-screen sound is the main warning: a door opening, footsteps, knocking or a thud upstairs. The player imagines worse than anything on screen.
- Escalate through the environment in the same room: the first visit is clean; the second adds a decal (handprint, writing); the third swaps a sprite (the painting now faces you); then the room is tinted.
- Scare density for this scope: one hard jump scare (full-screen picture plus sting plus shake) roughly every 8-12 minutes; soft scares (a shadow at the screen edge for 0.3 s, a portrait changing, a door slam) every 2-4 minutes; one long chase per chapter.
- Text pacing: slow text speed and one-second waits before key words, red-highlighted clue words, and a portrait change to 'scared' as the punchline instead of showing the monster.
- Light as pacing: rooms drop from 'lit' to 'dark radius' as the story progresses. A lamp or candle pickup becomes a breathing space.
- Safe rooms need a distinct palette (warm candle orange, soft music-box BGM) and a recognisable save object or creature, so the player feels the contrast when leaving.
- After every big scare, give 30-60 s of quiet exploration or a puzzle so the next tension rise is felt.

### Story structure

- (Unverified, from prior knowledge.) The opening is ordinary life in a cute style: a museum visit, kids exploring a rumoured mansion, a girl and her father at home, or a girl visiting a friend's house in the woods. The hook is a single visual break (the lights flicker and the crowd is gone; a door locks behind you).
- Chapter or area changes are marked visually: a new wall colour or tileset per area (Ib moves through colour-themed zones), a title or location banner, and often a full-screen picture with a caption.
- Lore comes through readable documents: diaries, letters, research notes and placards shown as item pages or full-screen paper pictures, often with handwriting fonts or smudges. These double as puzzle hints.
- Story beats are told with sprite acting, not new art: characters turn, jump with an '!' balloon, kneel or fall using extra sprite frames, with emotion portraits in the text box. Large CG illustrations are saved for deaths, the twist and the endings.
- Multiple endings with named title cards and an ending illustration (Ib, Mad Father and The Witch's House all have good, bad and true-type endings, as remembered). Endings depend on choices or on hidden actions taken throughout, so the visual and dialogue details the player noticed pay off.
- A twist revealed through art: an earlier portrait or face is redrawn with 'wrong' features, or a familiar sprite is shown in a new palette. The reveal reuses assets the player has already learned.

### Art and audio

- SCALE (unverified, high confidence): RM2000/WOLF games run at 320x240 with 16x16 tiles, characters 24x32 (3 frames x 4 dirs) and faces 48x48. RMXP runs at 640x480 with 32x32 tiles, characters about 32x48 with 4 frames per direction, and no default faces. VX/Ace runs at 544x416 with 32x32 tiles, characters 32x32 and faces 96x96. OneShot was built in RMXP but its art appears to be drawn at chunky 2x pixel scale (medium confidence). Lesson: keep one pixel scale everywhere and never mix resolutions, except deliberately for scare portraits and CGs.
- WALLS: one ceiling or top band (dark, often #1x1x2x-range near-black or a wood trim), a 2-tile wall face with wallpaper pattern and a 1-2 px darker baseboard row at the bottom, then the floor. Add a 1-px light highlight line where wall meets ceiling and a soft 2-4 px shadow cast onto the floor along the wall base. Doors take up both wall rows (16x32 at this scale).
- FURNITURE: tall objects (wardrobes, bookshelves, grandfather clocks, mirrors) are 1 tile of footprint but 2-3 tiles tall in sprite, overlapping the wall face. Every horror-relevant object needs a 'normal' and a 'wrong' version (painting with eyes open, doll turned around, mirror with a figure, blood-stained rug).
- CHARACTER SPRITES: chibi at about 2-2.5 heads tall, head about 12 px wide in a 16-24 px frame. Dark 1 px outline (dark hue, not pure black). Eyes as 1x2 or 2x2 dark clusters with an optional 1 px highlight. 3-frame walk cycle (1-2-3-2) with a 1 px bob in step frames. Hair and clothing colour is the character's identity colour; keep each lead's identity colour unique (Ib's red, Garry's blue, Mary's yellow is the classic pattern).
- EXTRA POSES on the sheet: kneel or sit, fall or collapse, look up, flinch with arms raised, hold an item up, lying down. Plus emote balloons drawn as 16x16 bubbles ('!', '?', '...', a sweat drop, a heart, a music note).
- FACE PORTRAITS: 48x48 (or 40x40) bust, 3/4 view, more detailed shading than sprites (4-5 shades per colour). Emotion set: neutral, smile, surprised, sad, scared, crying, angry or determined, plus 1 'wrong' variant (too-wide eyes, a blank stare, a shadowed face) for a corrupted character.
- MONSTERS: (a) a pursuer 1.5-2x player height with a simple silhouette and one exaggerated feature (giant head or mouth, staring eyes), drawn slightly cruder or more realistic than the cast so it looks out of place (the Ao Oni approach); (b) haunted art and objects (paintings, statues, dolls, mannequins) that move or change between visits (the Ib approach); (c) a full-screen scare portrait of each threat, drawn at about 3-4x scale (128-200 px tall) with high contrast and a limited palette.
- DREAD PALETTE (design recommendation, not sourced): mundane base in desaturated mid-tones, such as sick-green wallpaper #3e4a3a/#55644c, muted maroon #5a2e33, dusty cream #b8ab8c, wood floor #4a3426/#6b4a32. Shadows are blue-violet, not black (#1a1530, #2a2440). One saturated accent per scene, blood/rose #b0102a or #e0304a. Ghost or bone white #e6e2ee. Candle light #ffb040 with an additive glow. 'Other world' variant: shift the whole palette toward blue-violet and add red decals.
- CONTRAST STRATEGY: cute or pastel environments (The Witch's House) make gore stronger; plain, well-lit environments (Ao Oni) make an intruder stronger; clean gallery whites (Ib) make paint smears and colour accents stronger. Pick one strategy per area.
- SCREEN EFFECTS KIT: red flash at 0.2-0.3 alpha on damage; white flash plus thunder for lightning reveals; 6-12 frame shake for slams and the monster entering; a tint for state changes; desaturation for memories; instant cut to black after a scream; slow fade for story beats; a pixel-dissolve for crossing worlds; full-screen picture for jump scares and notes.
- TEXT BOX: full-width bottom box, 4 lines, dark translucent fill (about #0c0a14 at 85-90% opacity), 1-2 px light border with darker inner border, white text, red or amber for clue words, face at left, a small blinking 'continue' arrow at bottom right. No name label for unknown voices. A slow speed and pauses on '...' lines. Shake the text line on screams.
- TITLE SCREEN: minimal. Dark background showing one key image (the house, a painting, the rose or the monster's silhouette) and the title in a thin serif or hand-drawn font. A short menu: New Game / Continue / Quit (RM defaults are 'New Game / Continue / Shutdown' in XP and VX). Quiet music-box or piano BGM, or just ambience. Optional: the title image subtly changes after the player finishes the game or reaches certain endings (a common freeware touch, medium confidence).
- MENU: strip the RPG menu down to Items (icons plus a one-line description), Notes or Documents, Save/Load, Options and Quit. No levels or equipment. Show the diegetic health object (rose, candle or locket) in the menu corner.
- GAME OVER: a custom screen instead of the RM default image. Black or very dark red background, a short line of text or the title 'GAME OVER' in thin red letters, or a death CG first (Mad Father and The Witch's House emphasise unique death scenes). Short ME sting, then return to the last save. Let the player skip quickly, since deaths repeat.
- MUSIC: many Japanese freeware horror games use free-licence music and SE libraries rather than original scores. Commonly credited libraries (from memory, verify before citing) include Maoudamashii, DOVA-SYNDROME, Sound Effect Lab and similar sites, plus classical pieces, alongside some original compositions. The RPG Maker RTP stock tracks are usually avoided because players recognise them. For an original browser game, use synthesised or self-composed tracks: a music-box or detuned piano 'safe' theme, a low drone with a sparse 'dread' motif, a fast ostinato 'chase' with a recognisable first bar, and a sad piano 'ending'.
- AMBIENCE (BGS) per area: house creaks and a clock tick, rain on windows, wind, basement drip or hum, a near-silent 'void' tone. Key SFX: door open and close (a distinct pitch for 'the monster opened a door'), lock rattle, key pickup chime, page turn, glass break, heartbeat, scare sting (dissonant chord plus noise burst), footsteps, a creak under the player in specific spots.

### Criticisms and pitfalls

- (From prior knowledge, unverified.) Generic 'RTP look': stock RPG Maker tiles and characters make games feel interchangeable. The best-remembered games have distinctive custom sprites, monsters and a signature colour or motif.
- Ao Oni's monster is widely memed as goofy-looking when seen calmly. Its fear comes from pursuit, sound and surprise rather than detail. Show the pursuer mostly in motion, in short glimpses and in full-screen scare portraits, and don't let the player study it standing still.
- Overused jump scares become predictable and annoying; loud stings with volume spikes are a common complaint. Normalise SFX levels and limit hard scares.
- Darkness plus tiny interactables leads to pixel-hunting frustration. Add a subtle sparkle or a 1 px highlight on interactables, and an interaction prompt on facing.
- Colour-coded puzzles and identity colours can fail for colourblind players. Pair colour with shape or symbol (a rose vs. a different flower, a symbol on the door).
- Inconsistent pixel scale (mixing 2x and 1x art, smooth-scaled CGs, photos) breaks the cohesive retro look. Keep scale consistent and render with nearest-neighbour scaling.
- Unclear hitboxes during chases (large monster sprite vs. small collision) feel unfair. Make collision match the visible body, and give the chase a readable route.
- Text boxes covering the bottom of the screen can hide the monster or the exit during scripted scares. Move the box to the top or pause the threats while text is up.
- Instant-death design (The Witch's House style) can feel cheap without visual warnings and frequent saves. Telegraph the trap and checkpoint generously.
- Recognisable free stock music can break immersion for genre veterans who have heard the same track in other games. Originality matters, especially for the chase theme.
- Gore that's too graphic at pixel scale can read as comedic. These games often imply violence (a red splash, a cut to black, a sound) and save detail for a few CGs.

### Playtime and scope

(From prior knowledge, unverified.) Games of this type take roughly 1-3 hours for a first playthrough of a main route, with extra time for multiple endings. Ib, Mad Father and The Witch's House sit roughly in this range; Ao Oni's runtime varies by version. Art scope for a 1-2 hour game at this engine's scale (16x16 tiles, 416x240): about 4-6 visually distinct areas, each with a wall and floor palette and a 'changed' variant via tint and decals (about 25-40 rooms in total); 3-5 character sprite sets with 4-6 extra poses each; 6-8 portrait emotions for the lead and 3-4 for others; 1 main pursuer plus 2-4 lesser threats (haunted objects); 4-8 full-screen scare or CG pictures (including 1-2 ending illustrations); about 15-25 readable documents. Audio: 6-8 music loops (title, house or safe, dread, chase, sad, ending, bad end), 5-6 ambience beds and about 30-40 SFX. Reuse is the key economy, as in the originals: same room, new tint plus new decals plus one swapped sprite.

The main pattern across these games: a small cast (usually 1–4 key characters) is trapped in one closed, symbolic location (a mansion, a witch's house, an art gallery, a school, an apartment). ### Mechanics

- **Fragmented first-person diary as the main backstory channel**: (Unverified, from memory) In The Witch's House, pages of the witch Ellen's diary are spread through the house and found in progress order. They tell her story roughly in time order: a sickly, unloved child, a pact with a demon, killing people, and finally a wish for a healthy body and a friend. The last pages, found near the end, set up the twist that she has swapped bodies with the protagonist Viola. *Why it works:* Exploring is paid for with story. Because the pages are in the author's own voice and come in order, the player builds sympathy and dread at the same time. Holding back the key page until late lets the last entry change the meaning of the whole game. Lesson: give the diary an author whose identity or current state is itself the secret, and order the pages so the last one recontextualizes rather than just adds information.
- **Health or life stored in a fragile, symbolic story object**: (Unverified, from memory) In Ib, each character's life is a rose: red for Ib, blue for the companion Garry, yellow for Mary. Damage from traps knocks off petals, and water vases restore them. Enemies or paintings can steal a character's rose, and getting it back becomes a rescue objective. Garry's introduction is built around getting his stolen rose back (moderate confidence). *Why it works:* An abstract HP bar becomes something you can hold, take and threaten, with obvious symbolism (life, fragility, love). It also makes 'protect the companion' a puzzle about objects rather than combat. Reusable: tie HP to a themed object that the antagonist can steal or damage in a scripted beat.
- **Hidden relationship/affection flags that decide the ending**: (Unverified, from memory) Ib has several endings ('Promise of Reunion', 'Forgotten Portrait', 'Together Forever', 'Welcome to the World of Guertena', plus others). They depend on hidden choices and small kindnesses during play, on whether Garry survives, and on a final choice. Small mementos (a handkerchief lent to Garry, a lemon candy) pay off in the best ending. *Why it works:* The player's small, unremarked decisions add up to a verdict, which makes the ending feel earned and invites a replay to 'do better'. Mementos give the ending a concrete callback object. Caution: invisible point systems are opaque, and many players need guides. Give soft feedback (companion reactions) so the system can be read.
- **Named bad endings / 'Wrong Ends' as content, not just game over**: (Unverified, from memory) Corpse Party splits its story into chapters. Each chapter has several 'Wrong End' branches set off by wrong choices or failures, plus one True End that moves the story on. The Witch's House gives many traps their own short death scene. *Why it works:* Failure becomes a reward and a way to discover the rules. It shows what the world can do to you and gives completionists a goal. Lesson: write 3–6 short, distinct bad endings that each teach something or show a piece of lore, and make the true path clear in hindsight.
- **Victim records as collectibles (name tags, clippings, notes of previous victims)**: (Unverified, from memory) In Corpse Party, students trapped in the cursed Heavenly Host Elementary find the name tags of earlier victims, and collecting them unlocks extra content. Newspaper clippings and notes left by earlier visitors (including an investigator) explain the school's history and the curse. *Why it works:* Each collectible is a tiny tragedy with a name, which makes the danger feel real and backs up the main plot from the side. Lesson: earlier victims' traces are an ideal lore delivery device and can double as a completion tracker for the true ending.
- **Talking save point / knowing companion**: (Unverified, from memory) In The Witch's House a black cat acts as the save point and talks to Viola, sometimes cryptically. *Why it works:* The save point becomes a character and a vehicle for foreshadowing. A narrator figure who seems to know more than the player builds unease. Lesson: the save object can comment on story progress and drop hints, and can carry its own small mystery.
- **Perspective/identity twist built into the controls**: (Unverified, from memory) In The Witch's House, the player-character is not who she seems: by the end it is revealed that the 'Viola' you control is Ellen in Viola's body. The real Viola, trapped in Ellen's crippled body, is the crawling thing that chases you at the climax. At the exit, Viola's father shoots the crawling girl. *Why it works:* The twist hits the player's own complicity, because you were 'playing the monster' and fled from the victim. On replay, every early line and the final chase read differently. Lesson: if you plan an identity twist, seed fair clues (diary phrasing, NPC reactions, the protagonist's knowledge of the space) so a second play confirms it.
- **Secret 'non-participation' ending**: (Unverified, moderate confidence) The Witch's House has a widely cited secret ending: if the player waits at the starting point for about an hour without entering the house, the roses blocking the path go away and Viola can simply leave. *Why it works:* It rewards meta-curiosity and becomes word-of-mouth lore. It also comments on the story: only by entering did the tragedy happen. Lesson: a cheap-to-build joke or secret ending raises replay value and community buzz.
- **Parallel groups across overlapping spaces**: (Unverified, from memory) In Corpse Party, nine characters (students plus a teacher and a younger sister) end up split into small groups in different 'closed spaces' of the same cursed school. The player switches between groups by chapter, and each group finds complementary information. *Why it works:* Cutting between groups hides some information from each one and makes the same map feel new. It also lets the game kill characters without ending the game. For a 1–2 hour game, a smaller version could use two playable characters in parallel versions of one house.
- **Inherited-sin family secret delivered through a parent's workspace**: (Unverified, from memory) In Mad Father, Aya Drevis (a girl around 11) explores a family mansion while a curse fills it with the dead on the anniversary of her mother Monica's death. Her father Alfred's basement experiments turn people into preserved 'dolls'. Other figures include his assistant Maria and a mysterious masked man called Ogre. As commonly described, the true ending's epilogue suggests Aya inherited her father's obsession (low-to-moderate confidence on details). *Why it works:* The protagonist's love for a monstrous parent creates conflicting feelings. Because the true ending turns the horror back onto the protagonist, 'I escaped' becomes 'I became it'. Lesson: a family secret works best when the protagonist is implicated, either by blood or by complicity.

### Atmosphere and pacing

- (Design analysis, not sourced) Typical shape seen across these games: a mundane opening (visiting a gallery, a walk in the forest, moving apartments, a school after-hours ritual, an anniversary night) of about 2–5 minutes, then a sudden entrapment that removes the exit.
- The first 10–15 minutes set the rules: a lethal trap or chase, the save object, and the HP or death rules. Ib's petal loss and The Witch's House's instant deaths (unverified) teach caution early.
- Reveal cadence: one lore fragment per solved area or puzzle cluster. In a 1–2 hour game that means about 8–15 documents, with the strongest held for the last 20%.
- Midpoint escalation: a companion is introduced or endangered, or the monster's identity is partly revealed. Sympathy for the monster grows from here.
- Around 70–85%: the false-safety breaks (a companion lost or betrayed, or a friendly character revealed as dangerous, as with Mary in Ib, unverified). Then a final chase.
- The ending comes fast after the climax. Twists are delivered in 1–3 short scenes, followed by a quiet epilogue or a single image.
- Quiet stretches matter: long stretches of silence and exploration make the rare chase or jump scare work. Keep explicit scares under about 10% of runtime.
- Replay hooks: true or secret endings and collectibles make a second, shorter run likely. A 1–2 hour first run plus a 30–45 minute replay is a common target.

### Story structure

- (Unverified game facts; design lessons are analysis.) Small cast: The Witch's House is mostly Viola, the cat, Ellen (via diary) and the father. Ib centers on Ib, Garry and Mary. Mad Father centers on Aya, her father, his assistant, the dead mother and a masked stranger. Corpse Party is the exception, with about 9 main characters in a much longer game. Lesson: for 1–2 hours, use 1 protagonist, 1–2 companions, 1 antagonist presence and 1 absent figure (dead parent or previous victim) who is revealed through documents.
- One closed, symbolic location that stands for the theme: a witch's body and illness becomes her house; an artist's psyche becomes a gallery; a father's obsession becomes the mansion and lab; a school's buried crimes become a cursed school. Lesson: design the location as the antagonist's mind or history made physical, so exploring it is reading them.
- Sympathetic monster formula: a wronged or sick or lonely origin, then an act of desperation or a pact, then monstrous behavior that keeps the original wish (to be healthy, loved, free, remembered). The player learns the wish before the monstrosity is fully explained.
- Family secrets: a dead mother, a monstrous parent, neglectful parents, a sibling caught in danger (Satoshi's sister Yuka in Corpse Party). Lesson: make the secret implicate the protagonist (they benefit from it, inherit it or caused it).
- Twist types: (a) identity swap or the player-character isn't who you think (Witch's House); (b) the companion isn't human (Ib's Mary); (c) the protagonist inherits the villain's nature (Mad Father true end, low confidence); (d) the haunting mirrors the protagonist's own life (Crooked Man). Pick one main twist, foreshadow it at least 3 times, and make it re-readable.
- Ending branches commonly seen: Bad or Wrong endings (death or failure, often several), a Normal ending (escape, secret unresolved), a True or Best ending (needs saved companions, collected items, hidden flags or a replay), and sometimes a Secret or joke ending. For a 1–2 hour game: 1 true, 1 normal, 2–4 bad, plus an optional secret.
- True ending requirements that work: save the companion at a key moment, collect all lore fragments or victim tokens, make consistent kind choices (hidden points with visible reactions), or return a memento. Avoid requirements that can't be found without a guide.
- The 'true' ending is often bittersweet or darker than the normal one. It completes understanding rather than handing out reward.
- Document voice variety: a diary in the antagonist's voice, notes from previous victims, official records (newspapers, medical or school records), and object labels. Mixing voices lets the truth show through the contradictions between them.

### Art and audio

- (Unverified, general) Small chibi top-down or 3/4 RPG Maker or Wolf RPG sprites with large text-box face portraits. The gap between cute sprites and grim content is part of the effect.
- Event CGs or larger illustrations are used sparingly for key reveals or deaths (common in this genre). Use 3–6 full-screen illustrations at story peaks.
- Jump scares are usually a sudden full-screen image or close-up sprite plus a loud sound stinger, rationed so they stay effective (The Witch's House is known for them, unverified).
- Color as narrative code: Ib's colored roses mark identity and health (red, blue, yellow). Use a consistent color per character or life-object.
- Audio: sparse ambient loops, silence in exploration, distinct chase music that marks danger, and a soft melancholy theme for diary or reveal moments.

### Criticisms and pitfalls

- (Design analysis) Opaque hidden-point systems for endings (as in Ib's ending logic) push players to walkthroughs. Surface feedback through companion dialogue or small visual changes.
- Trial-and-error instant deaths with sparse saving (a common complaint about The Witch's House-style design) frustrate players. Pair lethal traps with clear foreshadowing and generous checkpoints.
- Walls of text: long diary dumps kill pacing. Keep entries short (40–120 words) and spread them out.
- Twists that aren't foreshadowed feel cheap. Twists over-foreshadowed with an obvious villain are spoiled early.
- Gore and shock as a substitute for meaning (a criticism often aimed at Corpse Party-style content) can fall flat. Tie each death to theme or lore.
- True endings locked behind obscure actions (idle timers, missable items) are good as secrets but bad as the only route to story closure.
- Copying risk: these tropes (body swap, living paintings, a doll-making father, a cursed school ritual) are strongly tied to their source games. Combine structural ideas rather than reusing any premise.

### Playtime and scope

These figures are from memory, unverified, and approximate. The Witch's House: about 1–2 hours. Ib: about 2–3 hours for one ending, more for all endings. Mad Father: about 2–3 hours. Misao: about 1–2 hours. The Crooked Man: about 2–3 hours. Corpse Party (remakes): much longer, at 10+ hours across chapters. Suggested scope for a 1–2 hour original: 1 protagonist, 2 companions or NPCs, 1 antagonist, 1 absent figure; 15–25 rooms in one location with 2–3 locked wings; 10–15 lore documents; 12–20 puzzles; 2–3 chase sequences; 1 true, 1 normal, 2–4 bad and 1 secret ending.
